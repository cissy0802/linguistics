Day 19 · Linguistics

Can language change what you see

Colour is the most laboratory-like of the three; space has been argued over for twenty years; time yielded the one genuinely clean piece of evidence
2026-09-15
Shuffle a few photographs of a man from young to old and ask someone to lay them on the ground in the right order. In most places people lay them from their own left hand to their right. In an Australian community called Pormpuraaw people lay them east to west: facing south that comes out left to right, and when the same person turns to face north it comes out right to left.

You are in a paint shop holding two blue swatches, hesitating for three seconds. Now imagine something else: if your first language treated those two blues as two unrelated words, the way 红 (hóng, "red") and 粉 (fěn, "pink") are two unrelated words for you — would you be any faster?

That is the ground linguistic relativity — the claim that language influences thought — is actually fought over today. Not "speakers of language L cannot think X"; nobody defends that version any more. The live question is much narrower and much harder: do the distinctions a language forces on you keep working when you are not speaking?

Three areas have been dug over for thirty years: colour, space, time. The three answers come back at completely different strengths, and that difference is itself the most informative thing here. Each gets the same ruler: how big is the effect, can you see it in a task that needs no words, and is it still there once language is blocked.

// 01

Russian has two names for blue, and it is worth a few dozen milliseconds

English blue covers the whole range from pale to dark, and so does Mandarin 蓝 (lán, "blue"). Russian has no such neutral superordinate: you have to choose between голубой (goluboy, the light kind of blue) and синий (siniy, the dark kind), the way no single English word covers both red and pink.

Winawer and colleagues brought this into the lab in 2007, with a task deliberately built to allow no talking: three patches on screen, one on top and two below, one of which exactly matches the top one. Press a key for left or right. Pure discrimination speed.

The result: Russian participants were faster on cross-boundary pairs — one patch falling on the goluboy side, one on the siniy side — and slower on within-boundary pairs, where both were light or both were dark. Show English speakers the same patches and the difference does not appear. And the smaller the colour difference, the clearer the effect.

The second step is what matters. They added a concurrent task: one group held a string of digits in mind while discriminating (tying up verbal resources), another held a spatial figure (tying up spatial resources). The digit group's cross-boundary advantage vanished; the figure group's survived. So the advantage is language joining in online, not different eyes.

Note the size of the effect: tens of milliseconds. Not "they see different colours" — one judgement got a shade faster. And the controversy runs the other way: Martinovic, Paramei and MacInnes re-ran the same task in two experiments in 2020 and found no cross-boundary advantage in reaction times, plus evidence that the siniy/goluboy boundary itself gets pushed around by how often and in what order the patches appear. This evidence is currently contested, not established.

While we are here, one widely circulated item deserves dismantling: the claim that the Himba "cannot see blue". The demo where eleven green squares hide one blue square and they supposedly cannot spot it comes from a 2011 television documentary, and no matching experiment appears in the literature; the published work (Roberson et al.) reports no discrimination advantage at the blue–green boundary their language does not mark, and an advantage at the boundaries it does mark.

// 02

There are people who never say "on the left"

"The cup is to the left of the book" hides something you do not normally notice: whose left. Change seats and the same scene needs a different sentence. That coordinate system, anchored to the speaker's viewpoint, is the relative frame of reference — which is to say, what you measure direction against.

Guugu Yimithirr, spoken at the Hopevale community in Queensland, Australia, does not use it. The language has four direction roots — gungga (north), naga (east), jibarr (south), guwa (west) — and all talk of location and motion runs through their inflected forms. Not "the ant on your left" but "the ant north of you". Tzeltal, a Mayan language of Tenejapa in Chiapas, Mexico, takes another route and uses terrain: ajk'ol "uphill" (roughly south, there), alan "downhill" (roughly north), jejch "across" — the same system for a coin on a table and for a village kilometres away.

The point is not the exotic-sounding "they have no words for left and right". The point is that to say one ordinary sentence in such a language you must know at every moment where north is. So Levinson and colleagues built wordless tasks: lay out a row of small animals all facing one way on a table, walk the person to a second table across the room, and after a delay have them lay it out again. Only two arrangements are possible — preserve "left to right relative to me", or preserve "actually facing north". Guugu Yimithirr and Tzeltal participants leaned towards the latter, Dutch participants towards the former.

Then comes the prettiest rebuttal in this whole area. Li and Gleitman ran the same table task with American students in 2002, varying exactly one thing: the laboratory blinds. With the blinds down and no view outside, nearly everyone chose the relative solution; with the blinds up and the scenery visible, or with the whole thing moved outdoors, people split roughly half and half. One more detail makes the case: in the conditions with a view, about 70% asked before starting which solution was wanted, against 20% with the blinds down. Not a word of language changed; the environmental cues changed, and so did the answer.

Levinson and colleagues hit back the same year, arguing that this conflates the geocentric frame with the object-centred one. The exchange has no clear winner: Li and colleagues later, in 2011, re-tested Tzeltal speakers with several different rotation tasks and did not reproduce the dominance of the absolute frame.

What survives is this one sentence: the frame of reference is not issued to you by your language, it is calibrated jointly by environmental cues and habit, and language's role is to make one of them obligatory, so that you have to maintain a running bearing estimate. That is much weaker than "their spatial cognition is different", but it can be measured. Evidence strength: cross-cultural non-verbal tasks, replication failures in both directions, samples of only a few dozen people.

// 03

Asked to lay time out on the ground, they laid it east to west

Time has no direction of its own, yet you can barely talk about it without borrowing space. English says "the week ahead". Mandarin says 上周 (shàng zhōu, literally "up week", meaning last week) and 下周 (xià zhōu, literally "down week", meaning next week) — a vertical axis. Same thing, two different axes.

Boroditsky reported in 2001 that English and Mandarin speakers making temporal judgements are primed differently by spatial cues in different orientations. That result then met three replication failures: Chen tried four times in 2007 and got nothing, January and Kako ran six experiments in 2007 with the same outcome, and Tse and Altarriba in 2008 likewise. Chen also pointed out something more basic — horizontal time expressions in Mandarin, such as 往前排 (wǎng qián pái, "move it forward") and 往后推 (wǎng hòu tuī, "push it back"), are actually more frequent than vertical ones.

In 2011 Boroditsky, Fuhrman and McCormick changed tack: no linguistic judgement at all, just have people point out a series of time points in the three-dimensional space around their own body. Both groups showed a left-to-right axis, matching writing direction; only the Mandarin speakers additionally showed a vertical one. The task itself needs no words, which makes this one of the firmer pieces here, though far from settled.

Aymara, a language of the Bolivian and Peruvian highlands, offers a different kind of surprise: it uses nayra — basically "eye, front, sight" — for the past, and qhipa — basically "behind" — for the future. Núñez and Sweetser in 2006 looked beyond the words and filmed the gestures: speakers point in front of themselves for the past and flick backwards for the future. One explanation they offer hangs on the language's evidential marking, which requires you to say whether you witnessed something: what you have seen goes in your field of view, what you have not goes behind your back. This is the only arrangement of its kind documented so far.

The cleanest piece is from Pormpuraaw. Boroditsky and Gaby in 2010 handed speakers of Kuuk Thaayorre a shuffled stack of cards — a man ageing, a crocodile growing, a banana being eaten — and asked them to lay them out on the ground in the correct order. They laid them east to west: facing south that runs from their own left hand to their right, turned to face north it runs right to left, and facing east it runs towards their own body. Nobody mentioned direction to them, and nobody told them the experiment had anything to do with bearings.

Why this piece is firm: laying out cards needs no words at all; one person turning to a new orientation produces a new layout, which makes them their own control; and no previously documented population laid time out this way — everything recorded before was body-relative. But do not read it as the strong version: they can perfectly well follow a left-to-right layout. What changed is the default, not the capacity.

🎒 Scenes · BigCat

  1. ① Working and living across languages: rescheduling a meeting across time zones. Someone writes that Wednesday's meeting has been moved forward two days — and native English speakers split on it, roughly 70% answering Friday and 30% answering Monday, a finding tested over and over since McGlone and Harding in 1998. The mechanism from "Asked to lay time out on the ground" says this is nobody being careless: time has no front or back of its own, front and back are a borrowed spatial axis, and both ways of borrowing it are legitimate — you move towards the future, or the future moves towards you. The fix is hard-edged: ban "forward" and "back" in scheduling messages and write the date and weekday. The metric is there for the taking — count the "so which day is it, exactly?" follow-ups over a quarter.
  2. ② Parenting and teaching: walking someone through a new tool remotely, you share your screen and say "click the one in the top left", "third row down" — while their window is a different width, their sidebar is collapsed, or the video feed is mirrored, so they either click the wrong thing or get stuck and say nothing. The mechanism from "There are people who never say 'on the left'" locates the problem: the relative frame presupposes a shared viewpoint, and once that presupposition breaks it fails without raising an error — you cannot see them clicking the wrong thing. The fix is to replace direction words with names: say the label on the control, say the tab name, say what to type into the search box. Count the "hang on, which one?" interruptions in a single walkthrough.

🌀 Crossing over · Notes from other fields

  • Distributed systems and AI · a machine writes the coordinate frame into a field of its own; a speaker can only fuse it into the sentence: robotics software carries a thing called a transform tree — map, chassis and camera each have their own coordinate frame, and every pairwise relation is recorded explicitly as a timestamped transform, so a coordinate travelling through the system always carries a tag saying which frame it was stated in. The mechanism from "There are people who never say 'on the left'" says the absolute frame is not issued by language but calibrated by environmental cues plus habit, with language merely making one of them obligatory, so a speaker must keep a bearing estimate running at all times. Put the two side by side and a conclusion falls out that you could not otherwise see: the cost is not in the vocabulary, it is in the upkeep. The classic transform-tree failure is not "north is unknown" but picking up a stale transform and using it anyway, emitting a confidently misplaced pose; whereas a human who has lost their bearings knows they have lost them and switches on the spot to "it's on your left". That is the testable difference: people have an "I am not sure" gear when crossing frames, and the transform tree has no such gear — it just extrapolates. Where this does not hold is treating the transform tree as a model of Guugu Yimithirr: the machine writes the frame as a field separate from the content, so the receiver can convert it themselves, while a Guugu Yimithirr speaker has no such field — the bearing is fused into the sentence, and without committing to one you cannot say the sentence at all. And obligatoriness is exactly the engine of the weak version, which has no counterpart in a system where the field is optional.
  • Complexity science · where a language cuts colour is not arbitrary, it is pressed there by the colour statistics of the environment: Gibson and colleagues in 2017 had speakers of many languages name colour chips, then computed how much information a word carries about which chip it was. From the Tsimane' of the Amazon to students in Boston, warm colours (yellows, reds) come through more reliably than cool ones (blues, greens); they then went and counted the colour statistics of natural photographs, and found objects tend to be warm while backgrounds tend to be cool. Zaslavsky and colleagues in 2018 recast the whole thing as a compression problem and found colour partitions across languages sitting close to optimal compression. The mechanism from "Russian has two names for blue" is that a category boundary is worth its few dozen milliseconds only near the boundary and only at the moment you have to make the discrimination. Join the two ends and the direction of causation flips: it is not that language carves the world arbitrarily and your mind follows, it is that the statistics of what needs separating in the world press the blade into that position. The testable difference follows: a language's colour boundaries should be predictable from the colour statistics its speakers are exposed to, and largely unrelated to genealogical kinship — two languages in one family, one of whose speakers dye cloth and the other not, should have boundaries that part company. Where this does not hold is the time scale: the compression model treats "what needs separating" as given and stable, whereas dyes and industrial pigments can rewrite it inside a few decades, while basic colour words are famously sluggish. So it explains the distribution across languages and fails to explain why any one language does not track its own needs.

Going deeper

Can "the effect is only tens of milliseconds" and "the effect is real" both be true?

They can, and that is the hardest thing here to state properly. Tens of milliseconds is not "small enough to ignore", it is "small enough that it must be described correctly": it is about judgement speed, not about what you see. To judge whether a write-up has overreached, see whether it dares to print the effect size.

If the frame of reference is calibrated by environment, does the gap between herders and office workers count as a language effect at all?

This is where those blinds really bite. Environmental cues alone are enough to make people switch frames, and communities speaking absolute-frame languages also tend to live where landmarks are clear and people move around all year — language and environment come bundled, and the field cannot separate them. Only one design can: find two communities with similar environments but different languages. Such pairs are rare, which is also why the samples here stay so small.

Between Mandarin's up-week / down-week pair and the direction of writing, which one is actually fixing the time axis?

Possibly neither is language itself. In those very datasets the left-to-right axis lines up with writing direction — and writing direction is a property of a script, not of a grammar. So "language influences cognition" has to say what "language" means: vocabulary? An obligatory grammatical category? Or the writing habits this language happens to come packaged with? Blurring the three is the commonest slide in this field.
