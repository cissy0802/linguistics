Day 26 · Linguistics

Languages Are Disappearing

The decisive step does not happen on the day the last speaker dies; it happened decades earlier, in somebody's kitchen
2026-09-22
You have probably heard that a language dies every two weeks. Nobody counted that. In 1992 someone estimated that up to 90% of languages might have no child learners by the end of this century; later writers spread that projection across a hundred years and got one every fortnight. Count the extinctions actually on record over recent decades and the rate is closer to one every three months. That is not the good news it sounds like — the decisive step comes forty or fifty years before the last speaker's funeral.

Lots of places have a language like this: the older generation uses it with each other and switches to something else with the young. Nobody announced the switch and nobody remembers when it started, and by the time someone notices and starts worrying, thirty years have gone.

Language loss usually gets told as a number (how many are left) and a moment (the last speaker). Neither is where it actually happens.

// 01

"One every two weeks" is a projection; the count says one every three months

That 1992 paper estimated that only about 10% of languages were safe long-term and that up to half might already have no children learning them. The author never said "one every two weeks" — later writers took "90% in a hundred years" and turned it into a rate, compressing an estimate about a distribution into a beat that sounds precise.

The other route is to count. The Catalogue of Endangered Languages tallied the languages documented as having actually fallen out of use in recent decades and got roughly one every three months. The same catalogue lists 3,116 still-spoken endangered languages, about 45% of those remaining, and a separately compiled database using its own scale arrives at about 44% — rare agreement for this field. The denominator is itself a judgement, though: whether a way of speaking counts as its own language or as one branch under another has no clean criterion, which was the conclusion of the topic on whose speech counts as standard.

More informative is a 2022 study that modelled 6,511 languages against 51 predictor variables and projected that about 1,500 could cease to be spoken by the end of the century. Two results cut against intuition. Contact with other languages is not itself a driver — multilingual coexistence can hold steady for a very long time. And places with more years of schooling show higher endangerment, not because education is harmful but because school usually runs in one dominant language, taking a key occasion away in the children's generation. Evidence strength: large-sample correlational analysis, not a causal experiment.

// 02

The dividing line is not the last speaker, it is the last child who learns it

In 1991 Fishman proposed a graded scale, and one level on it matters more than the rest: everyday transmission in the home, the neighbourhood, the community. His judgement was blunt — if that level fails, everything else amounts at most to biding time. So speaker counts mislead. Fifty thousand adults who speak it with no child learning it is closer to the end than five hundred speakers whose children are still learning; the first curve stays flat for forty years and then drops all at once. The standard definition of "endangered" is set on exactly this: the child-bearing generation no longer passes it on.

And it does not happen on one particular day. A language retreats occasion by occasion: out of official writing and the courts, then out of work and trade, then out of school, then out of conversation between friends, and out of the kitchen table last. The occasion held longest is the last to go and the easiest to misread — it is still there, so the whole thing looks like it is still there.

Usefully, there is an earlier signal. Dorian tracked the Gaelic of several fishing villages in East Sutherland, Scotland, from 1963 for four decades, and her 1981 book introduced the term semi-speaker: people who understand and can speak, but whose output has changed. This dialect built noun plurals several ways — changing a vowel in the stem, adding an ending, even subtracting a final sound — and the youngest group levelled almost everything to one suffix, or marked no plural at all. That morphological contraction arrives well before the population curve turns, and you can see it without any demographic data at all.

// 03

What is actually lost: three questions, three different answers

The first question belongs to the field. In 1963 Greenberg generalised from thirty languages and his first universal was that subjects almost always precede objects. In the late 1960s Derbyshire went to the Amazon to describe Hixkaryána, a Cariban language of Brazil with a few hundred speakers that nobody had written up before, and found its basic order puts the object first: "the jaguar grabbed the man" comes out as toto yahosïye kamara — first toto ("the man"), then yahosïye ("grabbed him"), and kamara ("jaguar") last. In a sample of 1,377 languages, only eleven are object-initial. So whether our "all languages …" sentences hold depends on a few dozen languages at the very edge — precisely the ones with the fewest speakers and the shortest time left.

The second question belongs to the community, and it is the one most often told badly. The usual line is that a worldview dies. But as the topic on the rise and fall of Whorf set out, the strong version did not survive, so that argument cannot carry the weight put on it — and it does not need to. What people themselves report losing is concrete: whether they can finish a thought with an elder, whether a ceremony can still be held, whether the place names still mean anything. There is also a much-cited correlation: a 2007 study across First Nations bands in British Columbia found that in communities where more than half of members reported conversational knowledge of their own language, youth suicide rates were close to zero, and about six times higher below that threshold. Evidence strength: a band-level correlation, not individual-level, and not causal — the original authors themselves compared it alongside six other measures of cultural continuity.

The third question is reversibility. Wampanoag had no speakers left after the nineteenth century, but it left a body of documents: a complete Bible translation from 1663, plus a stack of deeds, wills and petitions written in the language by Wampanoag people themselves. In 1993 jessie little doe baird started the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project and rebuilt the language from that material; her daughter, born in 2004, became the first native speaker in over a century, and the tribe now runs a full-time immersion school, Mukayuhsak Weekuw ("the children's house"). Documents cannot keep a language alive, but they settle the question of whether it remains possible at all.

// 04

Revival has worked, but what works is never restoration

The one case of a language going from no native speakers back to a community's everyday first language is Hebrew. The preconditions were unusual: it had never stopped being written and recited, its users were immigrants with no shared spoken language between them, and state power followed. The outcome is disputed too — Zuckermann argues that the modern Israeli language is a two-source hybrid, its vocabulary from Hebrew and its grammatical footing carrying the revivalists' native Yiddish. That the argument is possible at all tells you what "revival" means.

More reproducible is the language nest. In 1982 Māori communities opened the first kōhanga reo: preschool children spend whole days there in the target language only, looked after by elders who still speak it. Hawai'i followed the model with Pūnana Leo in 1983. A count at the time found fewer than fifty fluent speakers of Hawaiian under eighteen statewide, almost all of them on Ni'ihau; today the figure is in the thousands. For Māori, the 2023 census recorded 213,849 people reporting they could hold a conversation in it, about 15% more than in 2018 — self-reported ability, which is not the same as daily use.

And one example small enough to remember. Ned Maddrell died in 1974; he was the last person raised in a community where Manx was spoken. In 2009 UNESCO listed the language as extinct, and pupils at the Manx-medium primary school wrote in — in Manx — to ask what language they were writing in if theirs was extinct. The classification was later revised to critically endangered.

Put together, the successful projects all do the same thing: they put the language back into an occasion that happens every day without fail, and that occasion is usually the hours spent looking after small children. What fails is usually not underfunding but money spent only on class hours, textbooks and orthography — all of which sit at outer levels of the scale and never reach the one at home. That is the reverse test of the mechanism in "the dividing line is not the last speaker". As for the revived language not matching the old one: that was never a standard anyone could demand, since no language matches itself three generations on.

🎒 In Practice · BigCat

  1. ① Leading teams and organisations: some procedure that "people still know", except you cannot remember the last time anyone ran it end to end for the first time. The typical setting is two handovers later: the handover doc is still there, the report still says "captured", and when something breaks it is still the same one person who gets called. The test from "the dividing line is not the last speaker" applies directly — health is measured by new learners, not by the standing count, and the occasion held longest, here "when something breaks", is the last to go and the most misleading. The change: replace "how many people know it" with "how many people ran it alone for the first time last quarter", and if that number is zero, treat it as already stopped.
  2. ② Practice and inner life: something you used to do daily — journalling, sitting, a review, whichever — drifts from the morning to bedtime, then from daily to weekends, and finally to only when things are bad. It is always still there, so it never feels like it stopped. Same mechanism: what survives last is the emergency occasion, and that is the deceptive one. The change: do not add minutes, pin it back to something that happens every day without fail (after you shut the laptop), and pin one only. Switch the metric to how many distinct occasions it showed up on in the past fortnight, rather than total minutes.

🌀 Crossing Over

  • Distributed systems and AI · How big a backup is, and whether it can bring anything back, are different questions: there is an old rule in operations that a backup which has never been through a restore drill — actually reconstituting it into a running system — is not a backup. The restore path rots quietly as the surrounding environment drifts, and none of that shows up in the size of the archive. This runs the mechanism from the third question in "what is actually lost": documentation and use are separate things, and documentation buys exactly one thing, the answer to whether it remains possible. From which language documentation gains an acceptance test it does not currently have. The usual measure is volume — hours recorded, pages transcribed. A drill asks something else: has anyone, working only from this material and with no native speaker to consult, produced new speech the community accepts? Wampanoag is the case where that drill was actually passed. It also yields a testable prediction: whether recovery is possible should correlate less with the sheer volume of material than with whether the material contains natural conversation, because word lists and translated example sentences contain nothing about how people take a turn, interrupt, or make a joke. Where this breaks down is the acceptance test itself. Data restoration is binary — the bytes match or they do not. Language has no such baseline; what comes back inevitably carries traces of the revivalists' own first language, so whether it counts as success can only be settled by the community. Which is why whether modern Israeli is a continuation of Hebrew can be argued for decades, and nobody argues about whether a database restore counts as a restore.
  • Evolutionary biology · Both sides count what is left; only one side also counts how far apart things are: conservation biology stopped counting species alone a long time ago. There is a measure called phylogenetic diversity: sum the branch lengths on the evolutionary tree, and one isolated group with no close relatives is worth a whole cluster of near-identical species. Ranked that way, the priority list looks very different. This runs two of this topic's mechanisms together: "one every two weeks" showed that the denominator is itself a judgement, and "what is actually lost" showed that the cost to the field lands at the edges of the sample — so counting how many are left misses the point. The tree already exists on the language side; the comparative method from the topic on finding a language's relatives is the tool that builds it. Porting the measure across yields a prediction that can be computed directly and gives different answers from the current speaker-count ranking: language isolates (those with no demonstrated relatives at all, such as Basque) and small families down to one or two members rise to the top, while endangered members of large families fall back. Where this breaks down is irreversibility. In biology extinction is terminal; genes gone are gone. A language is carried by something learnable, which is why Manx could be moved back from "extinct" to "critically endangered". And precisely because such reclassification exists, priority-setting for languages has to include a quantity biology never needs: whether material survives from which it could be learned again.

Going Deeper

What separates "a language disappeared" from "a language turned into another one"?

Latin did not disappear; it became French, Italian, Spanish. The chain never broke, it just shifted a little each generation. Disappearance means the chain breaks: no next generation takes it up and no descendant language exists. The criterion sits on transmission, not on resemblance — which also shows why "the language is deteriorating" makes no sense here. Change is what being alive looks like.

However complete the record, what can it never buy back?

Documents give you forms, not when to say what. The people rebuilding Wampanoag had a Bible, deeds and petitions, and not one argument, one joke, one interruption. So a revived language is necessarily newly made at the pragmatic layer, mostly along the habits of the revivalists' own language — which is not a failure but the actual shape of the thing.

If preservation has to be prioritised, prioritised by what?

Speaker numbers, phylogenetic isolation, and whether usable material survives produce three quite different lists, and none of them is jointly optimal. More to the point, all three are external measures. What decides whether a language gets spoken in somebody's house tomorrow is not a ranking.
