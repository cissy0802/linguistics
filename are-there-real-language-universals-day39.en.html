Day 39 · Linguistics

Are There Real Language Universals

Across a thousand-plus languages, some combinations almost never show up. Is that a rule hard-wired into the brain, a habit languages inherited from their ancestors, or just speakers trying not to lose track? A language spoken by a few hundred people in the Amazon dragged the dispute into the open
2026-10-05
Among nearly a thousand languages checked, 456 put the verb before the object and use prepositions; 472 put it after and use postpositions; the two crossed combinations add up to just 56. Yet lay the languages out on their family trees, and this apparent iron law looks different in different families.

If you have ever learned Japanese or Korean, you have probably groaned: why is everything backwards? "Go to Tokyo" becomes "Tokyo to go". The odd thing is how tidy the reversal is: when the verb moves after its object, "to" moves after its noun as well. Languages almost never flip just half.

Tidiness like this convinced linguists that one shared system lies underneath every language. But "universal" can be read three quite different ways: something every language must have; something most languages tend to have; and something every human brain can handle but a given language need not use. Much of the heat in this debate comes from the two sides meaning different ones.

// 01

Where the object goes, the preposition follows

In 1963 Greenberg drew a set of universals from thirty languages, and the sturdiest kind took the form "if…, then usually…": if a language puts its object after the verb, it usually has prepositions (little words like in, to, from placed before the noun); if the object comes before the verb, it usually has postpositions (placed after the noun). English go to Tokyo has both the verb and to up front; Japanese 東京に行く (Tōkyō ni iku, literally "Tokyo to go") has both ni and the verb at the end.

A pattern like this is called an implicational universal. It does not say every language does something; it says a certain combination almost never turns up. Later typologists pushed the sample into the thousands and the diagonal held firm. For a more absolute claim — that the subject nearly always comes before the object — the topic on whether large models have made linguistics obsolete tells how an Amazonian language with the object first broke it.

In 2011 Dunn, Greenhill, Levinson and Gray asked the question differently in Nature. Counting languages has a loophole: four hundred Austronesian languages all descend from one ancestor, so their shared verb-first order may be a single inheritance counted four hundred times. They borrowed biology's method of drawing evolutionary trees and examined 589 languages in four families — Austronesian, Bantu, Indo-European and Uto-Aztecan — asking: when a language changes its verb–object order, does its adposition order change with it? The answer was that most dependencies between word-order features show up only in some families, and take a different shape in each.

The reply from the other side is strong too: four families are few, each family contains only so many word-order changes, and the statistical power is too thin to overturn a worldwide correlation. Today's rough consensus: the diagonal is a real tendency, but whether it is a law or a well-worn path is still open.

// 02

A language of a few hundred speakers became the exhibit in a whole lawsuit

Pirahã is spoken along the Maici River in the Brazilian Amazon by only a few hundred people; the other members of its Mura family are no longer spoken. Daniel Everett did fieldwork there for some thirty years. In 2005 he published the claim that the language does not put clauses inside clauses. Where English says "John's brother's house", Pirahã speakers would split it up: "Brother's house. John has a brother. It is the same one." His explanation was cultural: the community talks about what has been seen first-hand or reported by someone who was there, leaving no use for structures nested layer within layer.

This was a challenge to a very strong claim. In 2002 Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch proposed that the part of the human language capacity that is truly unique might be recursion alone — a structure that can hold another structure of the same kind, so that "I know you think he said…" can keep going down. If a fully working human language did without it, the hypothesis would at least need rewriting.

In 2009 Nevins, Pesetsky and Rodrigues wrote a long rebuttal with two core points. First, Everett's own 1986 grammar of Pirahã analysed some sentences as embedded clauses; in 2005 he changed the analysis, but not the material. Second, the "gaps" occur in other languages too: German's possessive placed before the noun also resists stacking a second layer, yet nobody says German culture limits German grammar.

In 2016 Futrell and colleagues went sentence by sentence through 1,149 sentences of natural speech that Everett had helped collect years before. They found no unambiguous embedding, but conceded that the corpus is small and was only shallowly analysed, so embedding cannot be ruled out. The evidence needs a clear label: one language, with almost all first-hand material from a very small number of researchers, hard for outsiders to check. And one more layer should not be forgotten: the much-cited "exhibit" is a living community whose language exists for living in, not for this dispute.

// 03

The sturdiest universal may be "don't let related words drift apart"

The Pirahã dispute finally jams at a fork. If "recursion is universal" means every language must use it, one language that truly does not refutes it. If it means every human brain has the capacity, Pirahã cannot refute it — Pirahã children raised elsewhere learn the embedded clauses of Portuguese like anyone else. Everett's side asks back: is a universal that no language could ever refute still an empirical claim about language? Both sides have a point, and the argument continues today.

Meanwhile another kind of universal has been quietly gathering evidence. In 2015 Futrell, Mahowald and Gibson took large annotated corpora of 37 languages and measured how far apart every pair of grammatically linked words sat — a verb and its object, say. In every language the average distance was shorter than a baseline with the word order randomly shuffled, and the gap widened as sentences grew longer. This is called dependency length minimization.

You do it yourself. In English both throw out the old rug and throw the old rug out are fine; but once the object gets long, speakers nearly always choose the first, keeping out beside throw instead of leaving it stranded behind a long string of modifiers. Look back at the figure: in English go sits next to to, in Japanese ni sits next to iku; the crossed combinations would wedge a whole noun phrase between verb and adposition. The diagonal may be a product of exactly this pressure.

On this account, universals live not in a grammar written into the genes but in speakers' limited working memory. It explains the tendencies, though not why some languages take the rare road anyway; and the universal-grammar side can reply that such pressure and innate structure may well coexist. Evidence strength: corpus statistics across 37 languages, pointing the same way as the finding, covered in the topic on which machines can handle which languages, that real texts rarely nest clauses more than three deep — but these are correlations, not direct proof of a mechanism.

🎒 Scenarios · BigCat

  1. ① Engineering and system design: In an architecture review someone says "every mature large system ends up split this way", then lists four or five companies. This runs on Dunn's question from "Where the object goes, the preposition follows": did these examples each get there independently, or were they passed down from one source — the same open-source framework, the same paper, the same group of people who changed jobs together? What to change: write the cited cases in a column, mark their shared origin next to each, and count only the independent "families"; then add one more question: did anyone take the other road, and how did it go?
  2. ② Writing and expression: A design doc contains "The rate-limit switch that the payments team added temporarily last quarter to handle the big sale and that nobody has dared touch since needs to be retired", and reviewers read it twice before finding what the subject actually does. This is the pressure from "Don't let related words drift apart": thirty words between subject and verb. What to change: when self-reviewing, look specifically at how far each subject sits from its verb; if it is more than a line, turn the long modifier into a sentence of its own — "Last quarter, for the big sale, the payments team added a rate-limit switch. Nobody has dared touch it since. It is time to retire it."

🌀 Crossing over · Interdisciplinary leaps

  • Distributed systems and AI · The brain's response to an "impossible grammar" is not failing to learn it but switching circuits: In 2003 Musso, Moro and colleagues had German speakers learn real rules of Italian and Japanese alongside a few "impossible" ones — for instance, the negation word always goes after the third word, turning Maria compra il caffè ("Maria buys the coffee") into Maria compra il no caffè. Participants learned the fake rules too, and accurately; the difference was in the brain: only while learning real rules did activation in Broca's area (a left frontal region closely tied to grammatical processing) rise with practice. This runs on the fork in "Don't let related words drift apart": test capacity and use separately. It yields a testable difference. The topic on whether large models learned grammar described small language models also finding such artificial languages harder — but comparing perplexity alone (how surprised a model is by the next word) cannot reveal a human-style circuit switch. The test should be: once a model has learned a count-the-word-positions rule, is that rule computed by the same internal units that handle real subject–verb agreement? In humans they are separate; if a model mixes them, it differs from humans on exactly this point, whatever its learning speed.
  • Evolutionary biology · Whether a trait is an adaptation shows in whether it changes together with another trait along the tree: Biology hit the same counting trap long ago: a hundred bird species sharing a trait may reflect a single evolutionary event in one ancestor. Felsenstein's 1985 solution was to look only at independent changes along the branches of the tree. This runs on Dunn's method from "Where the object goes, the preposition follows", and leads to a conclusion: if the diagonal comes from the "don't let related words drift apart" pressure, then in any family, a language that changes its verb–object order should change its adpositions fairly soon afterwards, and this "follow-on change" should point the same direction in every family — something that can be tested directly on the trees. But here it breaks down at the tree itself: genes rarely flow sideways between species, while languages borrow from each other massively, and neighbouring languages from different families can shift word order together, changes that bypass the family tree; and language family trees only reach back a few thousand years, far shallower than biology's, so there are fewer independent changes to observe — exactly the root of the doubts about the statistical power of Dunn's study.

Going deeper

If a perfectly ordinary language with "verb after object, prepositions before nouns" turned up tomorrow in some valley, what would that show — and what wouldn't it?

The grid already holds 14 such languages. One counterexample can topple an absolute universal but not a tendency. What is informative is how it got that way, and whether it has just been through heavy contact.

"The brain is capable of recursion; this language just doesn't use it" — how would you design an experiment that gives this sentence a chance of being falsified?

One approach is to look at whether speakers of that language show layer-within-layer structure outside syntax — in storytelling, planning, counting. If it is missing there too, the capacity claim has to shrink.

Dependency length minimization also appears in text generated by large models. Does that mean models are "afraid of losing track"?

Not necessarily: they learn from human writing, where the traces of the pressure are already present. To tell them apart, you would check whether a model trained on an artificial language that deliberately pulls related words apart shrinks the distances again on its own when generating.

If universals are mostly well-worn paths rather than hard-wired rules, what is left to explain the discomfort of a foreign language that feels "backwards"?

The discomfort comes from expectations your first language has already set, not from the target language itself. Japanese children learning English find go to Tokyo the backwards one.
