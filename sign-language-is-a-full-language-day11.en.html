Day 11 · Linguistics

Sign Language Is a Full Language

Not a hand-drawn substitute for speech — it has its own minimal contrasts, its own grammar, and its own aphasia
2026-09-07
A deaf signer after a stroke may be unable to draw a cube or see the left half of a room, and still sign with the grammar completely intact. Another may have excellent spatial skills and sign incoherently. What breaks is never "space." It is language.

You see two people signing at an airport — fast, economical — and you probably assume they are rendering spoken sentences word by word with their hands. That assumption has a direct test: put an American signer and a British signer together. The hearing people of both countries speak the same language; the two sign languages are mutually unintelligible.

So sign language is not a gestural edition of any spoken language, and it is not a universal pantomime either. Three things here: what the parts of a sign are, how space gets conscripted into grammar, and what breaks in a signer after a stroke — that last one overturned an assumption that used to sit on page one of the textbook.

// 01

Two countries with the same spoken language, two sign languages that cannot understand each other

American Sign Language and British Sign Language are separate languages, mutually unintelligible. The reason is historical: in 1816 Gallaudet went to Britain to learn their methods, was turned away, and went to Paris instead. A little over half of modern ASL's signs are cognate with nineteenth-century French Sign Language — a share below the threshold usually used to call two systems dialects of one language. Over a hundred sign languages have been documented, each grown inside its own deaf community, each with its own family relations.

If it is a language, it should come apart into parts. That is exactly what Stokoe did in 1960: he broke a sign into handshape, location and movement (orientation of the palm and actions of the face were added later). Whether the decomposition is right is tested by minimal contrasts — two signs differing in one part only, with different meanings. SUMMER and DRY in ASL are such a pair: index finger crooked, drawn sideways across, identical handshape and identical movement, differing only in location. Across the forehead it means "summer"; across the chin it means "dry." Exactly as English pat and bat differ by one sound.

This is the double articulation from "Language is not a dictionary": a small handful of meaningless parts, combining into an unbounded supply of meaningful words. It holds for sign language too — only the parts are not sounds, they are handshapes and locations.

There is a more intimate piece of evidence. When a speaker is stuck with a word on the tip of the tongue, what surfaces is usually the first letter, or the number of syllables. Signers get stuck too, and Thompson, Emmorey and Gollan collected these states: what surfaces is handshape, location and palm orientation, while movement is the part most often lost. Signs are stored by form as well, retrieval runs in the same two steps, and it jams at the same second step.

// 02

Space is not the backdrop; space is the grammar

A signer puts people into space first. Mention Ana, point briefly to a spot ahead and to the right, and Ana now stands at that point. Mention Ben, and he stands at another point ahead and to the left. From then on, referring to either of them does not require the name again — pointing back to the right spot is enough.

Verbs travel between those spots. The ASL sign for "give" starts at one point and lands on another: run it from Ana's point to Ben's point and it means Ana gives Ben; the same handshape, the same sign, run the other way, means Ben gives Ana. Direction carries who did what to whom — the job that word order does in English and that verb endings do in other languages.

You might object that this is just pantomime. A longitudinal study by Petitto blocks that reading. In ASL "you" is pointing at your addressee and "me" is pointing at yourself — as transparent as a symbol can get, so deaf toddlers ought to get it instantly. She followed two deaf children of deaf parents and saw this instead: around their first birthday they pointed at people and objects freely, then went through a stretch in which they avoided pointing at people altogether, and then began reversing — pointing at the other person while meaning "me." That is the same error hearing children make with you and me. Something that looks exactly like a gesture gets treated by the child as a symbol to be learned from scratch.

The controversy has to be stated. Whether these directional verbs count as agreement in the strict sense (the way a verb inflects for its subject) is still disputed. Liddell argues that the direction of pointing is continuous — it can land anywhere in space — unlike a discrete grammatical marker, and is better seen as gesture embedded in language. What is solid is that direction carries the who-did-what-to-whom; what is not solid is whether it is a discrete grammatical category.

Space buys one more thing: parallelism. Bellugi and Fischer had people tell the same story once in sign and once in speech, and measured something counterintuitive. An individual sign takes longer to produce than a spoken word, and noticeably more words than signs come out in the same stretch of time — yet both tellings take about the same total time and deliver about the same number of propositions. Handshape, location, movement and the actions of the face can each carry a piece of information at the same instant, whereas the mouth has only one serial channel.

// 03

What a stroke takes out is not space, it is language

If sign language were merely a good use of space, it should be tied to the side of the brain that handles space. Poizner, Klima and Bellugi followed a set of deaf signers with damage to one hemisphere, and the picture came out the other way round.

Signers with left-hemisphere damage became aphasic, in the same two types found in speech: some signed with enormous effort and with the grammatical parts stripped out; others signed fluently but chose wrong handshapes and produced content that did not add up. Meanwhile their non-linguistic spatial abilities could be essentially normal — copying a three-dimensional figure, assembling blocks. The most severely sign-aphasic of them had surprisingly intact spatial skills.

Signers with right-hemisphere damage showed the reverse. Spatial cognition collapsed: drawings lost their left half, figures could not be assembled. Yet the grammar of their signing was sound — including the part of the grammar made out of space: setting referents at points, running verbs between them, all correct.

That is a double dissociation: each can break on its own, so they are not one system. The brain is not sorting by "is this a spatial task" but by "is this language." The very same spatial coordinates go to the left when they are doing grammatical work and to the right when they are doing pictorial work. There is an independent line of evidence too: briefly anaesthetising the left hemisphere in a signer before surgery produces sign aphasia for those minutes.

Evidence strength should be marked: this is single-case and small-sample neuropsychology, on the order of dozens of patients. Later imaging broadly supports left lateralisation, but also finds more right-hemisphere involvement for the spatial components of signing than for speech — not a copy of the spoken pattern. And among right-hemisphere cases, one signer with devastated spatial ability told a coherent story while another with mild spatial problems could not hold the spatial frame of a narrative together. "The right hemisphere does space" is too coarse to work here.

// 04

The assumption that got overturned was on page one of the textbook

In 1960 Hockett published a list of "design features of language" — the properties meant to separate human language from animal communication. The list was cited for decades, and the first item on it was that language runs over the vocal-auditory channel.

Sign languages knocked that item out. The consensus now is that the language system and the physical channel it runs on are two different things: minimal contrasts, morphology, syntax, an acquisition order, aphasia — all of it holds when you move it to the hands. A 2025 review revising the list treats "vocal-auditory channel" and "arbitrariness" as obsolete entries.

The harder blow is that a sign language can grow from nothing. In a village in southern Israel, a form of hereditary deafness led to a local Bedouin sign language emerging over some seventy years. Sandler and colleagues found that by the second generation of signers a stable word order had already appeared — agent first, patient in the middle, verb last — which is not the order of the surrounding spoken languages, so it was not borrowed. Grammar does not need millennia. One generation is enough.

The assumption was never purely academic either. From the late nineteenth century onward, schools for deaf children in many countries taught chiefly through speech and restricted signing in the classroom, on exactly the grounds that it was not a real language. So "sign language is a full language" is not a courtesy. It is a string of falsifiable claims: minimal pairs exist, a fixed acquisition order with fixed errors exists, its own aphasia exists. Each was tested on its own, and each held.

🎒 In context · BigCat

  1. ① Engineering and system design: in a review everyone is gathered around one architecture diagram, every sentence is "this one" and "that thing over there" with a finger on the screen, and in the room everybody follows perfectly; two weeks later nobody reading the notes can say which boxes were changed. The mechanism from "Space is not the backdrop; space is the grammar" shows where the difference lies: once a signer sets a referent at a point in space, every later reference must return to that point, and pointing elsewhere is an error — those points are fixed and checkable, whereas the finger in a meeting room has no such rule and the points evaporate when everyone leaves. What to change: number and name every box on the diagram before the meeting and require speakers to say the name, with "this one" banned. Change the diagnostic too — not "did everyone follow," but: hand the notes to someone who was not there and see whether they can circle the changes on the diagram.
  2. ② Writing and communicating: you write a paragraph explaining three things that happen at once — who is waiting on whom, which leg times out first, what the other end is doing meanwhile — and it fills up with "at the same time" and "on the other hand" while the reader still cannot reassemble the picture. The signing-rate data explains why: individual signs are slower, yet telling the same story takes as long as it does in speech, because handshape, location, movement and face each carry a piece at the same instant, while text has one serial channel and can only splice parallel content with conjunctions. What to change: count how often "at the same time" and "meanwhile" appear in the paragraph; past two, move the parallel part into a diagram or a table and leave the prose only what the picture cannot say.

🌀 Crossing over · Connections beyond the field

  • Distributed systems and AI · change the physical channel and what gets rewritten is the structure, not the encoding: move a protocol onto a different link and the intuition is that the upper layer is untouched and only the encoding below changes; follow that intuition and sign language becomes "speech over a different link layer." The measurements in "Space is not the backdrop; space is the grammar" do not support that reading: individual signs are slower than words, yet the same story takes the same time and delivers the same number of propositions, because the grammar itself is reorganised to run in parallel — several parts each carrying information at the same instant rather than lined up in a queue. That yields a testable difference: a system that treats signing as "gesture recognition plus translation into a word sequence" should not fail uniformly, but should fail in a cluster on the structures that ride on the parallelism — spatial reference, the direction of verbs, the grammatical actions of the face. If the measured errors really do cluster there, what is being lost is channel-specific structure, not vocabulary coverage. Where this fails is the premise of layering itself: layering works because the same packet is handed across intact, whereas signed grammar is not spoken grammar in another encoding — ASL and BSL cannot understand each other and ASL is closer to French Sign Language. With no layer boundary where the same content is handed over, "different link layer" is only a figure of speech.
  • Buddhist thought · a name that looks like what it names still has to travel the symbolic road: Buddhist thought works over and over on the relation between name and thing — the name is a cut made by us, and it is not the thing. It is easy to grow an intermediate assumption from that: if a name resembles what it points at, the gap should be thinner. Sign language is a rare test site, because it holds far more resemblance than speech does. The mechanisms are the ones from "Two countries with the same spoken language" and from Petitto's longitudinal work: what surfaces when retrieval jams is handshape and location — form parameters, not what the sign resembles; and although pointing at your addressee for "you" is as transparent as it gets, deaf toddlers still go through avoidance and then reversal. The conclusion you could not otherwise see: resemblance buys no shortcut around the symbolic system; at most it is a handle for memory. Evidence strength: iconicity does help adults learning a second sign language and helps in some memory tasks, but helps little with the order of first-language acquisition, and that literature is still accumulating. Where this fails is reading any of it as support for or against "names obscure reality": that claim is about how to hold distinctions in practice, while the mechanism here concerns only the channel between a symbol's form and its referent. All sign language removes is the intermediate assumption that looking more alike means standing one layer closer.

Going deeper

If the direction of pointing is continuous and can land anywhere in space, can it still count as grammar?

Grammatical parts are usually taken to be discrete and countable, while spatial pointing is a continuous quantity. What would settle whether a continuous quantity inside a system is grammar or gesture — whether it breaks down together with the other grammatical parts in aphasia, whether children go through a fixed error period while learning it, or whether other rules can refer to it?

Sign aphasia rests on a few dozen cases and carries a very large conclusion about language being channel-independent.

To harden it, what would you do first: more cases, brain imaging, or a controlled intervention like the brief left-hemisphere anaesthesia? What does each of those add, and what is each of them constitutionally unable to add?

Deafblind people use a tactile form of signing, receiving with their hands on the signer's hands — the channel has changed once more.

With vision removed as well, which structures should survive unchanged and which have to be reorganised? Do the referential points in space still work, and where do the grammatical actions of the face go? If the observed reorganisation lands precisely on the parallel-information part, what does that say about the conclusion here?
