Day 16 · Linguistics

Children Can Invent Grammar

The children who built this language were not the ones who gave it grammar — that showed up in the next group
2026-09-12
"From nothing" is the part that needs fixing. These children did not arrive empty-handed; they arrived with a pile of private, mutually incompatible scraps. The rare thing is not that somebody pooled the scraps. It is that once the pile was handed to the next group of children, it became systematic on its own — the systematising happens at the receiving end, not at the inventing end.

Before 1977, most deaf children in Nicaragua stayed home. Schools taught no sign language, no deaf community had formed, and many children never met another deaf person in their lives. They communicated with their families through gestures they made up themselves — a system with a name: home sign.

That year a school for the deaf opened in the capital, Managua; enrolment reached a hundred by 1979. In 1980 a vocational school opened in Villa Libertad on the edge of the city, and by 1983 the two together held over four hundred students. The curriculum was lipreading and spoken Spanish, and that part went badly. The part nobody planned worked: on the buses and in the schoolyard, dozens of unrelated home-sign systems landed in the same pool for the first time.

In 1986 the linguist Judy Kegl was invited to look at what these children were doing with their hands. What she found was not a heap of gestures. It was a language.

Three things this time: which group of children the grammar actually appeared in (not the ones who built it), how you separate "practised longer" from "received it earlier", and how the same thing happened again in a village in the Negev — and swerved into a word order nobody around it uses.

// 01

They did not walk into that school empty-handed

Start with the starting point. A deaf child who has never met a sign language, growing up with hearing parents who do not sign, does not simply do nothing. Goldin-Meadow and colleagues followed such children for years and found that the gesture systems they invent carry consistent ordering: for who-did-what-to-whom, which gesture comes first and which comes second stays stable within a child.

Here is the crucial comparison: their mothers' gestures do not have these properties. A mother waves her hands while talking — loose, and different from situation to situation. So the structure in the child's hands was not copied. The harder result is cross-cultural: deaf children in Turkey and in the United States converge on the same way of packaging an event into a single action phrase, even though adult gesture habits in the two societies differ a great deal.

But home sign has a ceiling: it is one person's system, used in one household, with no second user treating it as something to be learned — it never grows the layered machinery a community language has.

So what happened in that school in 1977 was dozens of such systems meeting each other for the first time. What comes out of that? Senghas, Kita and Özyürek, in 2004, played a cartoon: a ball spinning as it rolls down a slope. Describe it.

Hearing Spanish speakers gesture with the hand swirling while it travels downward — manner and path fused into one movement. First-cohort deaf signers largely did the same. The second and third cohorts did not: they split it in two, first a "spin" held in place, then a "downward", one after the other.

// 02

The second cohort had not practised longer — they received it earlier

Now a finer piece of machinery. A sign language can aim a verb at a location in space that temporarily stands for a person: push the action "pay" out toward the empty spot held by "the one being paid", and point back at that spot later when the same person comes up. One location, one referent, and who-is-who never has to be spelled out separately.

Senghas and Coppola went looking, in 2001, for when this device appeared. Every cohort aims signs at spots in space, it turns out, but only the second cohort had turned it into a system — the same location bound stably to the same person. For the first cohort the space carried direction, not coreference.

The methodological move is the real point. "The second cohort is better" has a boring explanation: they were younger, so they learned faster. So the researchers separated "how old you were when you entered this community" from "how many years you have been signing" — they grouped each cohort by age at entry and compared across cohorts only within matching age-at-entry groups. The result: early-exposed first-cohort signers had more years of signing behind them, yet early-exposed second-cohort signers used the device more, and more systematically. Those extra years did not carry the first cohort past them.

What drives it is not fluency, but what you received while you were still young. Senghas later followed the same people across years and found individuals mostly settle after childhood, each carrying their own version forward — the change lives in the children, not in the adult users.

Evidence strength: this is a cross-sectional cohort comparison within one community, with no way to randomise and no way to run it again. The cohorts differ in more than age — by the time the second cohort arrived the community was several times larger, with far more people to talk to, and that is tangled up with age in a way nobody can untangle. This is the standing limitation of work like this.

It runs on the same line as the issue on what children's mistakes reveal: there, one child spread the "add -ed" pattern onto a well-memorised went, output more systematic than input. Here a whole cohort spread the previous cohort's scraps into a system. Same direction, scaled up to a community.

// 03

The same thing happened again in the Negev, and swerved into a different word order

In the Negev, in southern Israel, there is a Bedouin village of about four thousand people, more than a hundred and thirty of them deaf — a recessively inherited congenital deafness traceable to a few early families. Some seventy to eighty years ago a sign language grew up in the village on its own, and a good number of the hearing villagers use it too.

Sandler, Meir, Padden and Aronoff reported in 2005 that by the second generation of signers the word order had settled: subject–object–verb. To say that a woman feeds a child, the order signed is "woman child feed", with the verb resting at the end.

Why that matters: no language around it has that order. The Arabic dialect the hearing villagers speak is subject–verb–object, and so is Hebrew; Classical Arabic is verb–subject–object; the Israeli Sign Language already present in the region is not it either. So the order was not borrowed — it was fixed inside this language, and the point at which it was fixed was, again, the second generation.

Pull in another line: creole languages. Plantation economies threw together adults with different mother tongues, and among themselves they improvised a working means of exchange — a pidgin, with words but little settled grammar. Bickerton's language bioprogram hypothesis says the children of those adults turned the pidgin into a creole with a full grammar, and that the suspiciously similar parts across distant creoles come from a program people carry. His pivotal case was Hawai'i.

The other side struck back at exactly that case. Siegel, in 2007, re-examined the history and pointed out three things: formation spanned more than one generation; what the children received was not a rudimentary pidgin but a fairly stabilised one; and a good many features can be traced to the languages immigrants brought, Cantonese and Portuguese among them. DeGraff goes further: treating creoles as a class of languages exceptional in how they arise does not hold up as a premise at all.

To be clear: these are all full languages, merely young. A creole is not a simplified version of anyone, and the Negev sign language is not a local variant of Israeli Sign Language.

So "one generation is enough" has to be narrowed. What holds is this: a single pass through learners makes a system more systematic — seen once each in two independent places, landing both times on the second cohort or generation. What does not hold is that one generation of children builds grammar out of nothing. One is not enough. What is needed is somebody to take the previous generation's pile as the thing to be learned.

🎒 In the field · BigCat

  1. ① Leading teams and organisations: You built a system and then wrote its onboarding doc yourself. What came out is a pile of special cases: watch out here, that situation is an exception, we tried this once so don't. Six months later someone arrives, feels their way through it, and a month after that posts a rewrite — half the length, and actually followable. The mechanism in "the second cohort received it earlier" says that is no accident: only the receiver is forced to reconstruct a rule from incomplete input, while you hold every instance and never have to. What to change is the division of labour, not the doc. Stop scheduling "the author finds time to rewrite it" — what the author rewrites is still a table of exceptions. Have the second person who read it and actually got it running do the rewrite, with the author only checking facts. Change the metric too: not doc length or coverage, but how many questions the third newcomer has to ask before their first successful run.
  2. ② Investing and position sizing: You followed someone's publicly disclosed position changes for a year and distilled a table of four rules, which you now trade. Go back to what they actually said, though, and two of the four are things they never stated — you filled them in from a handful of rebalances. The same mechanism is the warning: the receiving end's output is more systematic than its input, and on your side the systematising is invisible because it wears the costume of "I remember them saying that". The cleaner the table, the more it tells you that you were the one filling gaps. What to change is the bookkeeping: next to each rule, record the number of independent occasions you saw it hold — not how confident it feels, the count — and mark the ones with only an instance or two as "this one is mine". The action to stop is citing their name in support of those rules; once cited, they stop being open to refutation, and they are exactly the ones that should go first.

🌀 Out of bounds · cross-disciplinary notes

  • Distributed systems and AI · whether self-training decays or builds depends on the gate in the middle: A body of work now worries about "model collapse" — train the next model on a model's own output and after a few rounds the output grows monotonous. Kirby, Cornish and Smith, in 2008, chained people up to do the same thing and got the opposite: each generation learns part of an artificial language and teaches it onward, and the language becomes more structured with transmission, ending up with recombinable parts that no generation set out to design. The mechanism is the one from "the second cohort received it earlier": the systematising happens at the receiving end, and its driver is that the receiver sees less than the whole language — forced to fill gaps, and what fills a gap is a rule. That yields a comparison you can actually run: one self-training chain fed the full output of the previous generation, another fed a strict subsample with communicatively unsuccessful samples filtered out, measuring description length and compositionality per generation. If the mechanism is right, collapse belongs only to the first arm — making it a property of the feeding regime, not of self-training as such. Where this does not hold is the source of the bottleneck. In the lab the bottleneck is scarce data; in Managua it was not — by the time the second cohort arrived there were four hundred-odd students and far more people to talk to than the first cohort ever had. The narrow aperture on the human side is an age window, and a model has no age window — so whatever the chain produces cannot be evidence that the human effect requires a critical period.
  • Complexity science · "agreeing on words" and "growing a grammar" are not the same engine: Centola and Baronchelli, in 2015, had nearly a hundred people online paired off in a naming game: look at the same face, each write a name, win a little money if the names match. Where everyone can meet everyone, a global consensus emerges on its own with no central coordination; switch to a network where you only meet neighbours and local conventions form smoothly while that one global name never settles. This end is pure negotiation, all adults, nobody learning, and the product is a shared vocabulary; Kirby's chain of learners has no negotiation at all, and its product is combinatorial structure. Held against this issue's narrowing, the two engines come apart: for the Negev sign language to exist, the vocabulary half needed a village bumping into itself repeatedly, and the grammar half needed a second generation taking the first as input. From that you can preregister a criterion: the naming-game paradigm will not grow a grammar however far you scale it, because it hands the meaning space over ready-made — one face, one name, no parts inside to carve. Where this does not hold is the reverse half: a chain of learners will not grow a device like "this location in space stands for that person" either. That device exists to track who is who across a stretch of discourse, and it needs an interlocutor listening in real time, not a person quietly learning.

Going deeper

If grammar always shows up in the second cohort, what did the first one contribute?

What they contributed cannot be measured by asking whether it had grammar: a public system, in use, worth learning. That is exactly what home sign lacks — not structure, a second person. So the question becomes how many users are enough. The first few dozen students in Managua were; one child in one household is not; where the line between them falls, neither case can say.

What actually supports "it was not borrowed"?

Its word order differs from every language around it, which looks solid. But how far does "around" extend — the next village, television, people who worked in the city? An argument by elimination is only as strong as the candidate list, and the list is drawn up by people. Turn it around: had the order matched the local Arabic dialect, would that have settled it as borrowing? Then the evidence only ever falsifies borrowing; it never establishes spontaneity.

Is "output more systematic than input" too handy a general property to hand to learners?

It has been seen in two places: one child's overregularization, one cohort's systematisation. But the two differ by two orders of magnitude in timescale, and the layers in between — a household, a classroom, a village — have barely been measured. A shape that recurs across scales may be one mechanism, or several leaving similar traces. To tell them apart, you first need a prediction that holds at only one of the scales.
