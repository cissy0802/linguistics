Day 23 · Linguistics

Finding a Language's Relatives

Resemblance is not evidence; systematic correspondence is. A nineteenth-century craft can reconstruct words nobody ever heard, and be checked against languages dug up later
2026-09-19
Persian has a word pronounced bad, and it means "bad". English bad sounds the same and means the same. The two are not related in any way: trace the Persian word back and you reach an earlier wat. Meanwhile Spanish hijo and French fils sound nothing alike, yet they are two descendants of one word. Working out who is related to whom has almost nothing to do with looking alike.

You have probably heard that English and German are relatives, or that Chinese and Tibetan are. The question is on what grounds. There are over seven thousand languages; take any two and their wordlists will throw up a few pairs that sound alike. Yet the claim is solid enough to be used for inferring where ancient people moved and when they set out. How is that step taken?

The procedure is called the comparative method: using living descendants to reconstruct an ancestor for which there is no record. Three things about it — what it accepts as evidence, how it reconstructs a word nobody has ever heard, and where it breaks down. The last one matters most: only a method that knows its own boundary is entitled to say any of the above.

// 01

Resemblance does not count; correspondence does

First, throw out resemblance. Besides the Persian bad, there is another pair every course covers: Greek theos and Latin deus both mean "god" and look alike, yet they are not relatives, each descending from its own root. The genuine relative of Latin deus is Greek Zeus, the one that looks nothing like it. Words that look alike need not be family, and family need not look alike.

What counts is a wholesale, repeatable correspondence. Latin word-initial f turns up in Spanish as an h that is written but not pronounced: filius against hijo ("son"), facere against hacer ("to do"), ferrum against hierro ("iron"). These three are not coincidences; the f in that position went down the same road across the vocabulary, while Italian and French did not follow (figlio, fils). So the test is not whether two words resemble each other, but whether one sound in one position stands reliably opposite another.

The prettier part is that even the exceptions obey rules. Latin focus ("hearth fire") gives Spanish fuego with the f intact, yet focaria, built on the same root, gives hoguera ("bonfire") with h. The difference lies in what follows: the f stays before the diphthong ue and changes elsewhere. A rule that looks broken usually just means the conditioning has not been written out in full.

The same test also throws the loanwords out. Spanish fiesta ("festival") and febrero ("February") keep their initial f because they were not handed down continuously; they were borrowed back from written Latin after the change had already run. English shows it more starkly: the native words are father, fish, foot, while the cognates borrowed from the Latin side are paternal, piscine, pedal — f on one side of the same slot, p on the other. A word's first consonant tells you which door it came in through. Correspondence is not only evidence; it is also a sieve.

// 02

Take several daughter languages and reconstruct a word nobody has heard

The craft itself is not mysterious. Line up cognates — sets of words descended from one ancestral form — by meaning: how each language says "son", how each says "do". Then pull out the sounds that line up within each set and check whether they line up the same way in other sets. Finally choose an ancestral form such that the fewest and most natural steps lead from it to every descendant. Direction matters too: stops between vowels tend to soften, and rarely the reverse.

Why trust this reasoning? Because one family lets you check the answer: the ancestor of the Romance branch left a great deal of writing behind. You can pretend not to know Latin, reconstruct from Spanish, Italian, French, Portuguese and Romanian, and then open the books. It matches — with one telling offset. What comes out is not the classical Latin of the textbooks but something closer to the version people actually spoke. The comparative method always gives you the latest common ancestor of the daughters, not the written standard of the same era.

Nor is the craft confined to Europe. The sounds of Middle Chinese were likewise reconstructed. The 切韻 (Qieyun, a rime dictionary of 601) spelled each syllable using two other characters and sorted the characters into classes, but never said how those classes were pronounced. Bernhard Karlgren filled in sound values by triangulating three sources: those classes, the pronunciations of modern regional varieties, and the Chinese readings borrowed wholesale into Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese. The reconstructions are still being revised; the procedure is the same one.

The hardest test came in 1879. Working on alternations of vowel length in Indo-European, Ferdinand de Saussure argued that a class of sounds found in no language then known had to be posited for those alternations to make sense. For half a century the claim was treated as an outsider's eccentricity. Early in the twentieth century, tablets in Hittite — an ancient language of Asia Minor — were deciphered, and in 1927 Kurylowicz pointed out that a Hittite consonant turns up exactly in the positions Saussure had predicted. A reconstruction is not a restatement of existing material. It is a commitment: if a new relative is ever unearthed, this is what it will have to look like.

// 03

Push back about eight thousand years and it stops working

If the method is this powerful, why has nobody joined all the world's languages into one tree? Because the signal runs out. Every thousand years another batch of cognates is replaced, further sound changes pile on top, and correspondences get harder to recognise; meanwhile chance look-alikes keep occurring at a fixed rate. Past some depth the real signal drops below the noise — the problem is not that resemblances are missing, but that they no longer show anything.

How large the noise is can be computed, and computing it is exactly what Ringe did in 1992: estimating on pure probability how many pairs of similar sound and similar meaning two wordlists should throw up, and arriving at a figure far above most people's intuition. His target was the practice of laying wordlists from a hundred languages side by side and hunting for similarities by eye. The dispute has never entirely settled, but one side of it is clear: resemblance with no systematic correspondence behind it cannot be told apart from accident.

So the ceiling sits at roughly eight thousand years — the figure usually quoted is 8000 plus or minus 2000, with an upper bound no deeper than ten thousand, partly because the oldest securely established families are about that old. Deeper proposals, such as joining Indo-European and Uralic into a larger Nostratic grouping, are not in the position of having been refuted; they cannot reach the evidential bar. At that depth, what would support them and what chance alone produces look the same.

The current frontier borrows statistical tree-building from biology, coding the presence or absence of cognates as characters and producing dated trees. Indo-European origins have been argued over on this basis for two decades: one camp roots the family in Anatolia and follows the spread of farming, the other on the East European steppe, following migration evidence from ancient DNA. In 2023 Heggarty and colleagues published a root age of about 8120 years in Science and proposed a hybrid in which each side holds a stretch. The strength of that evidence should be stated plainly: it is a model posterior, and it shifts when cognate judgements or rate assumptions change.

Shifts by how much? Look at Sino-Tibetan. In 2019 two teams applied comparable methods to the same family, reached similar conclusions about geography, and landed on about 7200 years and about 5900 years — more than a millennium apart. The method is real and so are the error bars. The ceiling is the same kind of fact: not a shortfall of craft, but information eaten by time. No algorithm, however ingenious, reads out a signal that is no longer there.

🎒 Scenarios · BigCat

  1. ① Engineering and system design: two services contain two suspiciously similar utility classes and you need to decide whether one was copied from the other. Most people diff the behaviour, but identical behaviour shows little: there are only so many correct ways to write the thing. What carries information is the shared arbitrary choice — the same misspelled constant name, the same oddly worded log line, the same unexplained 37-second timeout. This is the mechanism from "Resemblance does not count; correspondence does": the test rests on arbitrariness, because there are countless arbitrary ways to write something and the odds of two people colliding are tiny. The change to make is concrete — in this kind of provenance work, do not diff behaviour, grep for the arbitrary bits. The standard to hold yourself to: before you go to the other team, do you have one piece of evidence that nothing but a shared source explains?
  2. ② Working and living across languages: reading Japanese material and guessing meanings because the characters look familiar works most of the time, until you hit a word like 手紙 (tegami, "letter" in Japanese — the same two characters mean "toilet paper" in Chinese) and never notice you got it wrong. The mechanism from "Take several daughter languages and reconstruct a word nobody has heard", run in reverse, is the fix: what is stable is not similarity of meaning but systematic correspondence of sound. Characters that ended in -k in Chinese generally end in -ku or -ki in their Sino-Japanese readings — 学 (xué, "study" → gaku), 石 (shí, "stone" → seki), 客 (kè, "guest" → kyaku) — while those that ended in -t take -tsu or -chi — 一 (yī, "one" → ichi), 日 (rì, "day" → nichi). The change to make: memorise the correspondence rather than the feeling that a word looks familiar, and when a new character compound turns up, predict its reading from the correspondence before you look it up. The measure is your hit rate — after twenty words, if it has not climbed, you are still guessing from resemblance.

🌀 Crossing over · Connections beyond the field

  • Evolutionary biology · Language trees and species trees run on one inference, but the clock on the language side has to be binned by word frequency: biologists building trees also refuse shared retentions and accept only shared innovations — two species both having a backbone says nothing about how closely they are related, whereas both having some specialisation found nowhere else does. That is the same logic as "Resemblance does not count; correspondence does". The molecular clock can put dates on such a tree because the mutation rate per site is roughly constant, and that constancy has a physical basis, unrelated to how much a given gene gets used in daily life. Language has no such basis: Pagel and colleagues in 2007 combined four corpora with 200 basic meanings across 87 Indo-European languages and found that the more frequently a word is used, the more slowly it is replaced — a single factor accounting for about half the variation in historical replacement rates. From which follows a conclusion you would not otherwise see: the time axis of a language tree can only be right if it is binned by frequency, and one uniform clock will systematically misdate some nodes. That is also why this issue's ceiling is so soft — half-lives differ across words by an order of magnitude. Where it does not hold is the tree shape itself. Moving genes between species is mainly a bacterial affair and can be treated as noise in most groups; in language, borrowing is the norm, and a language can borrow from several neighbours at once. So "which family does this language belong to" and "how tree-like is this dataset" are two separate questions in linguistics, the second one turned into a directly computable quantity (the kind of index Gray, Bryant and Greenhill pushed in 2010). Species trees have no step that must be done that way.
  • Distributed systems and AI · A neural network can guess Latin back, but it cannot guess a sound that exists in none of the descendants: Meloni, Ravfogel and Goldberg trained a sequence model on exactly this task in 2021 — feed in cognates from the Romance languages, have it output the Latin form, over eight thousand entries — and it beat the automatic methods that came before, with internal representations that line up with documented sound changes. The craft appears to have been automated. But "Take several daughter languages and reconstruct a word nobody has heard" points to a structural difference: the comparative method does not want the form that most resembles the descendants, it wants the form from which all of them can be derived, even if that form left no direct trace in any of them. A model of this kind has an output alphabet consisting of the sounds seen in training, and its objective is to bring the predicted form as close to the target as possible. The testable difference is therefore blunt: remove Hittite from the data and watch whether the model ever volunteers an extra element nobody has seen in some position. It will not, because that only raises the loss. Saussure did precisely that, and paid for it by being treated as a crank for fifty years. Where it does not hold is the scope of that human advantage: it depends on change being conditioned and enumerable. Once the residue answers to no phonetic conditioning at all — the kind described in "Language changes like a river" — the criterion of the most economical ancestor is unusable, and human and model alike are left counting frequencies.

Going deeper

Is comparing "basic vocabulary" circular — what licenses the assumption that such words are hard to borrow?

It is not circular, but it is not insurance either. Body parts, numerals and pronouns are empirically less likely to be borrowed, and that is all: English they, them and their were borrowed from Old Norse, although pronouns are usually held to be the hardest of all to borrow. The wordlist only lowers the noise. What actually carries the weight is still correspondence, since loans tend to give themselves away in the sound matches — like the Spanish f that never became h.

Did the reconstructed ancestor have dialects of its own?

The comparative method assumes by default that the ancestor is a single homogeneous point, and no real language is homogeneous. If the ancestor had already split into dialects when the daughters diverged, reconstruction flattens those differences and yields a system that contradicts itself; in practice such contradictions are often written up as "several variants coexisting in the proto-language". The recent statistical models that let documented ancient languages sit on the tree as sampled points are aimed at exactly this layer.

An isolate like Basque: does it have no relatives, or did all its relatives die?

Almost certainly the latter. Every language has ancestors, and nearly every one once had related neighbours. "Isolate" is a statement about the evidence we hold, not about the language itself — its cousins either disappeared or separated so long ago that they fell past this issue's ceiling.
