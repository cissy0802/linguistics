Day 29 · Linguistics

A Word Is Known by the Company It Keeps

A lazy idea written down in 1954, and still the foundation of nearly every language technology you use
2026-09-25
Four sentences, containing a word you have certainly never seen. A bottle of tezgüino is on the table. Everybody likes tezgüino. Tezgüino makes you drunk. We make tezgüino out of corn. You now roughly know what it is — no dictionary, no definition, nobody holding up a bottle. The method you just used happens to be the only method a machine has; and the most honest thing about it is that it cannot tell "hot" from "cold".

What surfaced in your head after those four sentences was probably not a definition but a position: it stands beside wine, beer and rice wine, a little further from water and milk, and very far indeed from cars. (Tezgüino is real — a corn-based fermented drink among some Indigenous communities in northwestern Mexico.)

The interesting part is not that you guessed right. It is that you guessed right holding nothing: no picture, no taste, nobody pointing. All you had were the other words around it — and that was enough. The idea has a name, the distributional hypothesis. It is half a century older than deep learning, every text-processing system in use today is built on it, and it carries a blind spot that has never been removed.

// 01

You have never looked up tezgüino, yet you know it is drinkable and will get you drunk

Unpack those four sentences and the cues are all there: "a bottle of" makes it a liquid with a volume; "on the table" places it at meals; "everybody likes" makes it a consumable rather than a tool; "makes you drunk" alone pushes it into the alcoholic class; "out of corn" supplies the raw material. Together they fence off a small region — and beer and rice wine occupy that same region. What you know is who it stands next to.

In 1954 the linguist Zellig Harris turned this into a claim: how far apart two words are in meaning can be read off how far apart their environments are. Three years later J. R. Firth left the endlessly quoted line — you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Both say the same thing: stop asking what the word points at in the world, and look at its neighbours. At the time this was only a fieldwork trick, but it had a side effect that took sixty years to matter: neighbours can be counted, and pointing-at-the-world cannot.

Now the blind spot. Complete this: "the coffee is ___." Hot and cold both fit. "This coat is very ___": expensive and inexpensive both fit. "He drove ___ for two hours": east, west, north, south, take your pick. Words with opposite meanings turn out to be the most freely interchangeable of all, because they demand almost identical neighbours.

So what the distributional hypothesis measures has to be stated precisely: it measures whether two words can be swapped into the same slot, not whether they mean the same thing. Near-synonyms are interchangeable; so are antonyms; the method files them together. This is not a bug in some program but a consequence of the idea itself — and there is a whole line of research devoted to injecting "these two are opposites" back into the result afterwards, which pushes the problem down only a little.

// 02

Squeezing a table of fifty thousand by fifty thousand, almost all of it zeros, into three hundred numbers per word

How do you turn neighbours into something a machine can compute? The crudest way is also the earliest: build a huge table, one row and one column per word, each cell holding how often the two occur together within a short span of text. Tezgüino's row carries counts under "bottle", "drunk" and "corn" and a zero under "screwdriver"; wine's row looks much the same. Similarity is how alike two rows are.

The trouble is that the table is both enormous and almost entirely empty: most words are rare, most word pairs never co-occur at all, and under one percent of a fifty-thousand-square table carries anything.

So it gets squeezed. In 1990 a group working on document retrieval proposed latent semantic analysis: compress that vast empty table into a few hundred numbers per word. The squeeze pulls together rows that keep the same company, so "car" and "automobile" land near each other even if they never shared a sentence. In 1997 Thomas Landauer and Susan Dumais put it through an exam: trained on some thirty thousand encyclopedia articles, about 4.6 million words, it took the synonym section of the TOEFL, eighty four-way multiple-choice items. It scored 64.4%, while applicants from non-English-speaking countries averaged around 65% on the same items. Read that number carefully: it shows that counting co-occurrence alone can reach a population's average on one particular item type. It does not show that the program knows English — synonym choice is the home ground of a distributional method.

In 2013 the approach changed from count-then-compress to guess-and-adjust. Hide a word in a sentence, have the program guess it from its left and right neighbours, and when it guesses wrong, nudge the string of numbers attached to each word. After a few billion guesses, that string is the word's position. This is word2vec, and it became famous for something that looks like a conjuring trick: take the numbers for "king", subtract "man", add "woman", and the nearest landing point is "queen".

The trick is overrated. In 2016 Tal Linzen pointed out something plain: the standard procedure first removes the three input words from the candidate answers, because otherwise the nearest position is usually "king" itself. Much of the success therefore comes from "queen" already being close to those three, not from a route from male to female stored inside the vectors. The phenomenon is real; the explanation was inflated.

The same year brought a sturdier result: in spaces trained separately on English and Spanish, number words have a similar internal arrangement, and so do animal words, so a single transformation lines the two up well enough to guess dictionary entries that were missing. The alignment problem from the machine-translation story reappears here wearing a different face — there it was counted sentence pairs, here it is the shape of two spaces.

// 03

Is it learning what words mean, or how people use them

In 2017 Aylin Caliskan and colleagues took the standard psychological test for implicit association and ran it, unchanged, on word vectors trained from web text, publishing the result in Science. The findings lined up one by one: flower words sit closer to pleasant words and insect words closer to unpleasant ones, a much-replicated human result; and how close an occupation word sits to female terms correlates positively with the actual share of women in that occupation.

The right reading is not "the vectors are biased" but that co-occurrence is all they have, so they cannot separate two things. In "nurse occurs near female terms", half is a fact about the labour force and half is an inertia in how people write, and counting treats the two alike. Separating them needs exactly what this method lacks by design: a world outside the word list.

There is a more basic limit too. One word gets one string of numbers, and that string can only be the average of all its uses. Mandarin 打 (dǎ) is a hard case: 打电话 (dǎ diànhuà, "make a phone call"), 打水 (dǎ shuǐ, "fetch water"), 打毛衣 (dǎ máoyī, "knit a sweater"), 打车 (dǎ chē, "hail a taxi"). The neighbours fall into several clusters, and one averaged position resembles none of them. English "bank" has the same problem, the riverside and the institution sharing a spot. Later models give every occurrence its own position, so the same 打 (dǎ) lands in different places in different sentences.

And people? People use distribution too — as the statistical-learning story described, infants who cannot yet speak are already tracking which syllables keep each other's company. The difference lies elsewhere, and it is concrete enough to compare. In 1978 Susan Carey and Elsa Bartlett told three- and four-year-olds "bring me the chromium tray, not the blue one, the chromium one"; the children brought the olive one, and a week later still linked the word to that colour. This is fast mapping, and it does not run on counts — it runs on the child seeing two trays and ruling out the one that already has a name. The difference is therefore testable: a word usually needs dozens to hundreds of occurrences before it has a decent position in a co-occurrence space, and a child needs one; conversely, no person will ever read the millions of documents a count can cover. (On replication: a single exposure yields partial knowledge, and how long it lasts is still disputed.)

In 2020 Emily Bender and Alexander Koller drew the line harder with a fable. An octopus taps an undersea cable and eavesdrops on two people ashore; in time it can hold up its end of the conversation seamlessly, having never seen anything on land. Then one day it is asked how to fend off a bear with a few sticks, and it has nothing. Their claim: meaning cannot be learned from form alone. The counter is not weak either — most speakers of "inflation" or "justice" have likewise never seen what those words point at, and use them perfectly well. The argument is unsettled. What is certain is narrower: the distributional hypothesis gives a word's position inside language, and says nothing about where that position is pinned to the world.

🎒 In Context · BigCat

  1. ① Writing and explaining: in that design document of yours, "task" on page 3 means one submission by a user and on page 9 means one unit of execution inside the scheduler. You can tell them apart; your reader has only the method from the tezgüino section, and the neighbours in both places happen to be "submit / queue / retry on failure" — the distributions overlap, so he cannot separate them. What to change: before you freeze the draft, pull out every key term and read its sentences in one column. If one word's neighbours fall into two clusters, split it into two words; if two different words have fully overlapping neighbours, delete one. Measure the number of interruptions in review that begin "which sense do you mean here".
  2. ② Leading a team: three weeks in, the new hire is already saying "let's put this behind a gradual rollout" and "drop a metric in while you're there", fluently, until the day it is used somewhere it plainly does not apply. Their route in was co-occurrence, and distribution only gives you what a term resembles, never what it excludes — the antonym blind spot, showing up here as a boundary that was never learned. What to change: in the team's jargon list, do not write definitions. Write one line of "what it is not" plus the nearest confusable neighbour ("a gradual rollout is not an A/B test; the first exists to limit blast radius"). Measure how often the new hire gets corrected the first time they use a term on their own.

🌀 Crossing Over · Connections Beyond the Field

  • Buddhist philosophy · "cow" means "not a non-cow" — a theory of meaning built purely on difference, written down in the sixth century: Buddhist epistemology has a doctrine called apoha, literally "exclusion". Dignāga (roughly 5th–6th century CE) argued that the word "cow" does not point positively at a class of real things; it is constituted by excluding every non-cow. The mechanism is the one running through this issue: a word's position is fixed by what it differs from, not by content it carries inside itself. That yields a conclusion you would not otherwise reach — the failure to separate "hot" from "cold" is not sloppy engineering but a consequence of the theory itself: if meaning is purely differential, two words whose contrast sets coincide must be indistinguishable. It even suggests a test: rank words by how far their contrast sets overlap, and that ranking should predict vector distance, most sharply for antonyms. Where it breaks down is the ground of the exclusion. In apoha the excluding is purposive — later thinkers anchored it in whether something achieves a practical effect, milk or ploughing, which is why cows form a class at all. Counting has no purpose; all it can exclude is words that never showed up together. Apoha can say why cows and horses are two classes; a distributional method can only report that the two words are similar.
  • Psychology · put words in a space twice, once with coordinates from people and once from text, and the two must disagree precisely on antonyms: in 1957 Charles Osgood built the semantic differential — have people rate a word on scales running between poles like good–bad, strong–weak, fast–slow, then factor-analyse. Three dimensions keep falling out: evaluation, potency, activity, and they recur across communities speaking many different languages. The contrast is clean: both methods compute a position for each word, but one takes its coordinates from human responses and the other from co-occurrence in text. That gives a testable difference with a known direction — the two sets of coordinates must disagree most on antonyms, since "good" and "bad" nearly coincide in a co-occurrence space while sitting at opposite ends of the evaluation dimension. Antonymy is not unmeasurable; it needs a different instrument. Where it breaks down is coverage: the semantic differential has only three dimensions and all of them are affective, so it cannot separate "cat" from "dog" (both mildly good, mildly active), which a co-occurrence space does easily. Which one carries more information depends on which kind of difference you are asking about.

Going Deeper

If neighbours define meaning, and neighbours are words too, is this not circular?

It is, and that is what critics point at most often: the system is internally consistent with no point pinned to the world. One reply is that circularity is not uselessness — a map with only relative positions still gives directions, provided you are standing somewhere on it. The other reply is more modest and more honest: co-occurrence statistics never claimed to deliver meaning. They deliver similarity, and similarity was always relative.

If a language has little text, is it doomed to poor word vectors?

Scarce material does mean poor vectors, but be clear about what kind of problem that is. The amount of text reflects whether publishing, administration or religion created a demand for writing in that language; it has nothing to do with its structure or expressive power. Reading "the model does badly on this language" as "this language is deficient" mistakes institutional history for linguistic fact.

Does the octopus fable survive if you replace the octopus with a person born blind?

This is one of the strongest lines of objection. Someone who has never seen colour still uses colour words correctly and can discuss red being darker than pink, and nobody says they do not understand those words. The other side has an answer too: that person still lives among objects, temperatures, weights and other people's reactions, so language is not their only input. The question is therefore not whether eyes are required, but how much besides words is required — and nobody has produced that quantity.
