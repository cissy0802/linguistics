The Grid Your Brain Draws Over Sound

You don't hear the sound itself — you hear which box it landed in, and the boxes were drawn before your first birthday
2026-08-30
The two p sounds in English pin and spin are a few dozen milliseconds apart, and that gap is the entire difference between two different words in Mandarin. English speakers never hear it, their whole lives. Not because their ears are worse — because their brains never drew a line at that spot.

Find an English speaker, have them hold a palm in front of their mouth, and say pin, then spin. The p in pin puffs air onto the hand; the p in spin barely does. Ask whether those two p sounds are the same and they will almost certainly say: same thing, both p.

But the difference on your palm is real, and it is exactly what separates two Mandarin words: 八 (bā, "eight") has the unpuffed one, 趴 (pā, "to lie face down") has the puffed one. The same pair of sounds is a boundary between two words in one language and simply doesn't exist in another.

It runs the other way too. 西 (xī, "west") and 诗 (shī, "poem") are two different words in Mandarin, while English has only one sh in that region — so English learners of Mandarin routinely file both into the same box. The ear receives the same thing; what gets heard differs. In between sits one step: sorting. What follows is what that grid looks like, why some people have no line between L and R, and when the lines get drawn.

// 01

The difference you can't hear is one you flattened yourself

Two terms first. A phoneme is the smallest unit of sound a language uses to tell meanings apart. The actual sounds that come out of a mouth, if swapping one for another never changes which word you said, are variants of a single phoneme — allophones. The test is crude: can changing just this one sound give you a different word?

Run the test. In Mandarin, swapping the initial of 八 (bā, "eight") for the initial of 趴 (pā, "to lie face down") changes the word — two phonemes. In English, putting the unaspirated p of spin where the aspirated p of pin belongs sounds odd, but nobody thinks you said a different word — one phoneme, two variants. The same pair of physical sounds, entirely different status in the two languages.

There is a very direct piece of evidence that the grid exists. In 1957 Liberman and colleagues synthesised a string of sounds spaced evenly along one axis: a clear b at one end, a clear p at the other, a dozen or so steps in between, each step exactly the same physical distance from the last. (The axis is the quantity from the earlier issue on the invisible choreography in your mouth — how many milliseconds separate the lip release from the onset of voicing.) Then they asked people to do two things.

The first was to listen one at a time and report b or p. If the ear reported the physics honestly, you would get a gentle ramp. What you get is a step: the first several stimuli are called b a hundred percent of the time, and after one particular step they flip to p a hundred percent of the time, with almost no transition zone.

The second task is the telling one: compare stimuli in pairs and only answer "same or different". Adjacent steps are equally far apart everywhere on the axis, so in principle every pair should be equally hard. In fact: two sounds on the same side of the step are nearly indistinguishable, with accuracy close to guessing; step across the boundary and suddenly the difference is obvious.

The result has been reproduced countless times; it is bedrock. But its strong version — that within-category differences are literally inaudible — was overturned by finer methods. In 2002 McMurray, Tanenhaus and Aslin put four pictures on a screen and had people click the word they heard, while nudging the word's initial consonant around inside a category. The word people reported never changed, but their eyes flicked toward the competing picture — and the closer to the boundary, the more they flicked, continuously.

So the more accurate statement is: what got flattened is your judgement, not the signal arriving in your head.

// 02

The ear is fine; the line just isn't at that spot

That Japanese speakers struggle with English r and l is famous enough to be a stereotype. It is worth telling because one experiment eliminated the "bad ears" explanation cleanly. First, where the two sounds differ: the shape of the vocal tract makes energy pile up at certain frequencies, and those bands are called formants. The main difference between r and l sits in the starting height of the third formant from the bottom — low for r, high for l — after which each glides into the following vowel.

In 1975 Miyawaki and colleagues synthesised a string running from ra to la, changing only that formant's starting point, evenly spaced. Japanese listeners doing the discrimination task produced a nearly flat curve; English listeners produced a clear peak at the boundary, the dashed line from the previous section.

The second step is the key one. They pulled that formant out on its own and stripped everything else away. What remains sounds like a chirp — nothing like speech. Same two groups, same physical differences, discrimination task again: the two curves nearly coincide. The Japanese group hears it just as well as the English group.

That settles it: the auditory system itself is not different. The difference appears at the step where the sound is handled as language — failing to hear it is not a failure to receive it, but a matter of what box it gets filed into once received. Evidence strength: a clean controlled experiment with synthesised speech, redone many times since.

And the Japanese side is not "missing a sound" either. Japanese has a single liquid phoneme, produced close to a tap — the tongue tip flicks once against the ridge behind the teeth, like the fleeting tt in American English butter. It sits between English r and l, so both English sounds fall into that one box. Korean likewise has a single liquid phoneme with two variants: an l-like sound in the syllable coda, as in 달 (dal, "moon"), turning into a tap between vowels, as in 다리 (dari, "leg" or "bridge").

The street runs both ways, so don't look down only one of them. English has one sh across the palatal-to-retroflex region; Mandarin draws two lines there — x as in 西 (xī, "west") and sh as in 诗 (shī, "poem") — and English speakers learning Mandarin have to add a line where they never had one. Every language has contrasts other people cannot hear. Nobody has the better ears.

Can a line be added later? Yes, but not cheaply. In 1991 Logan, Lively and Pisoni trained Japanese speakers to identify English r and l using materials from multiple talkers in multiple phonetic contexts; after a dozen-odd sessions, scores rose clearly, and the gains transferred to unfamiliar talkers and new words. Evidence strength comes in two layers: that training works at all is solid; but the specific recipe — more variety is better — failed to replicate in a 2022 study with 166 participants, whose authors concluded the advantage, if real, is much smaller than believed. Right direction, dosage undetermined.

// 03

Before their first birthday, babies switch off a batch of distinctions

When does the grid get drawn? Earlier than most people guess, and the process runs backwards: not learning new ones, but switching off the unused. In 1984 Werker and Tees tested infants in English-speaking homes. At six to eight months they could tell apart the two Hindi t sounds (one with the tongue tip against the back of the teeth, one with the tip curled up to the front of the hard palate), and also the two ejective stops of Nlaka'pamux, a Salish language of British Columbia (one made with the tongue body at the soft palate, one further back at the uvula). English-speaking adults can do neither. Then they tested ten-to-twelve-month-olds: also gone. In those few months, the net had closed.

But use the word "lost" carefully. Over that same span, discrimination of the contrasts their own language uses gets better. This is not decay, it is reallocation — the technical name is perceptual reorganization: a system that only has to handle English moves its resources onto the handful of lines English actually uses.

How is it learned? Nobody tells a baby which sounds count as one box, and nobody hands out labels. In 2002 Maye, Werker and Gerken played six- and eight-month-olds two minutes of sounds from one continuum, with a single difference between groups: the shape of the distribution. One group heard the endpoints often and the middle rarely (two peaks); the other heard the middle most (one peak). Afterwards only the two-peak group could tell the endpoints apart. Two minutes, no feedback, distribution shape alone — which incidentally explains the narrowing itself: a line your language does not use never produces two peaks in the input.

Run it in reverse: pour in a new language's distribution before the window closes, and a box should be addable. That is exactly what Kuhl, Tsao and Liu did in 2003. Nine-month-old American infants got twelve sessions of twenty-five minutes each, with live Mandarin speakers reading and playing with them. Afterwards they could tell apart the Mandarin contrast between q as in 七 (qī, "seven") and x as in 西 (xī, "west") — a pair English does not use. The same material delivered on a television screen, or as audio only, in the same room, for the same duration, on the same schedule, left infants indistinguishable from a control group that had never heard Mandarin at all. Evidence strength: a small-sample experiment whose direction has since been supported more broadly, though the conditions have been narrowed — a 2018 study found infants do pick up some of it from video when a peer is present. One controversy worth flagging: the narrowing itself is solid, but the perceptual magnet effect Kuhl proposed to explain it (the most typical sound of your language pulling nearby sounds toward it) is still argued over, and in 1998 Lotto and colleagues showed it is quite sensitive to task design.

One last thing to be clear about: the grid is not a prison. The training studies in the previous section show adults can still move it, and the eye-tracking result from the section on differences you flattened yourself shows the within-category information was never destroyed in the first place. The real constraint is how you move it: redrawing a line takes a changed input distribution plus external feedback, not listening harder — the "you" who is listening harder is the very layer that got flattened.

🎒 In Context · BigCat

  1. ① Engineering and system design: during an incident review you scroll the latency dashboard back and find that P99 climbed from 210 ms to 340 ms inside the "amber" band, over two weeks, with not one message in the on-call channel; everyone moved only on the day it crossed red. This is not nobody looking. It is the mechanism from the section on differences you flattened yourself, running on a dashboard: once a continuous quantity is painted into three bands, within-band change stops being reported as change — it was amber every day, so every day was "the same". Two things to change: make the alert condition a within-band slope (seven consecutive days of monotonic rise fires) rather than a line crossing; and print the number inside the coloured block instead of only colouring it.
  2. ② Parenting and education: someone asks you whether putting on foreign-language cartoons will give a small child a head start on pronunciation. Kuhl's data give an unusually hard answer: same material, same duration, same room — a live person works, television and audio-only do not. The mechanism is the section on what babies switch off before their first birthday: redrawing the grid runs on the input distribution and on feedback inside an interaction, not on exposure hours. What to change is the metric — not hours played per week, but how many back-and-forth exchanges with a live person happen per week, with the budget following that number. And state the boundary: these studies measured infant phoneme discrimination only. They do not license the larger claim that video can't teach a language.

🌀 Crossing Over · Interdisciplinary Threads

  • Distributed systems and AI · which distinctions get dropped is computable before training: what the Maye result says is that whether a distinction survives depends not on anyone teaching it, but on the shape of the input distribution along that axis — two peaks survive, one peak doesn't. That turns something normally learned at evaluation time into something computable beforehand: plot a histogram of your corpus along any measurable dimension, and unimodal-versus-bimodal already predicts whether the model will keep that distinction. It also yields a concrete human-machine difference. A person who has "lost" a contrast did not throw the signal away — a few milliseconds of within-category variation still shifts how strongly competing words are activated. A system that quantises its input into discrete symbols loses the within-category difference at the quantisation step, full stop. The test: inject a perturbation inside a category too small to change the decision. Human reaction times and fixation proportions should shift continuously; the model's output should be bit-identical. Where this breaks down is the "distribution is destiny" half: an infant's input distribution is whatever the environment supplies and is nearly impossible to intervene on, while a model's training distribution is chosen and can be made bimodal at will. The same mechanism is a developmental fact on one side and a revocable decision on the other.
  • Buddhist thought · names carve continuous experience into boxes: there is a version of this claim that holds literally in speech — a continuous axis gets cut into two plateaus and a cliff, and within-plateau differences stop being reported. That is precisely the mechanism from the section on differences you flattened yourself. But phonetics measures more finely, and the finer measurement sharpens the claim by a notch: what gets cut off is not perception, it is report. The two sounds you call "the same" already moved your eyes differently. So if "names obscure reality" means the information is destroyed, this is a counterexample; if it means the information cannot reach the layer you can speak from, this is the cleanest evidence available — and the difference is practical, because the first is unrecoverable while the second can be routed around with any measure that bypasses verbal report. That also predicts something you can act on: checking whether you are being led by a concept by asking yourself "am I really seeing this clearly?" is useless, since you are querying the flattened layer itself. Where this breaks down is plasticity: phoneme boxes grow out of the input distribution before age one and are not under attentional control — no amount of concentration lets you hear the two p sounds in pin and spin. The discriminating mind that Buddhist practice talks about is explicitly held to be workable by trained attention. The break falls exactly on "can attention change it", and it is a useful break: it predicts that what actually corrects pronunciation is not listening to yourself more carefully but changing the input distribution and adding external feedback.

Thinking Further

If within-category differences still do work, how much is left of "can't hear it"?

The report says same; the eye movements say different. So is "this person can't tell these two sounds apart" a statement about their perception, or about what they can report? Switch to a measure that doesn't rely on verbal report, and how many cases of "can't tell" turn into "can tell but can't say"?

What exactly got switched off at age one?

If adults can partly get it back through training, what was switched off clearly isn't the receptor. Was the output of the sorting step wired shut, or was the channel to the reporting layer cut? The two stories predict different things — the second predicts that non-report measures should always show a residue.

Live works, television doesn't — which ingredient is it?

At least three candidates: a live person tunes what they say to the child's attention; a live person supplies contingent feedback; a child is simply more engaged when someone is present. Can the three be pulled apart experimentally? Add real-time responsiveness to video — a live person on a video call — and where should the effect land?
