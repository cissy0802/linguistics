Writing Was Invented

People have been talking for tens of thousands of years, yet writing has been built from scratch only three or four times — and each time the turning point was not better drawing, it was someone letting a sign record a sound
2026-10-03
The earliest tablets cannot be read as a sentence: a grain, a quantity, a personal name, and no word order at all. They are receipts. The step that carried writing over the threshold was someone using the picture of an arrow to write "life".

A clay tablet from over five thousand years ago, with a few signs pressed into it: a grain, a quantity, a personal name. You can identify them one by one, and still not read it as a sentence — there is no word order, nothing says who did what to whom. It is a receipt.

That is easy to file under "writing was still primitive". The interesting move is the opposite one: bookkeeping does not need sentences at all. For signs to be able to write any sentence, something apparently unrelated has to happen first — somebody has to take a drawn thing and use it to record the thing's sound.

The Sumerians did exactly that. After that step, what can be written down is no longer limited to what can be drawn: anything that can be said out loud can be written.

// 01

The real dividing line is not how well it is drawn, it is someone using the picture of an arrow to write "life"

Start on this side of the threshold. The earliest writing generally accepted as such comes from Uruk in Mesopotamia, around 3300 BCE, and it is overwhelmingly accounting: a commodity, how much, to whom, the total. It usually cannot be read as a complete spoken sentence — word order, who did what, has no place on the tablet. Its own earlier ancestor was not even signs but small clay tokens sealed inside a ball, one token per sheep.

The step across the threshold is a sign switching from what it depicts to what it sounds like. Sumerian for "arrow" was ti, and "life" sounded close enough, so the sign that drew an arrow was borrowed to write "life". The trick is called the rebus principle. English plays the same game: draw an eye to mean the word I.

Chinese kept an unusually clean trace of this step. The oracle-bone form of 其 (qí) drew a winnowing basket — the tool for tossing grain — and that was its original meaning. But it was borrowed to write a near-homophonous pronoun, 其 (qí, "its"), so successfully that a new character 箕 (jī, "winnowing basket") eventually had to be made for the basket: the original owner was evicted from its own house.

Doing all this from scratch is currently credited only three or four times: Mesopotamia around 3300 BCE, Shang-dynasty oracle bones in China around 1200 BCE, and Mesoamerica around 500 BCE (the oldest surviving body is Zapotec stone carving). Whether Egypt around 3200 BCE counts as independent is argued over — it is close enough to Mesopotamia that what travelled may have been the idea that signs can record speech. On evidence strength: the dates and the independence claims are archaeological inference, and new finds move the numbers.

Every other script inherited the idea, and what gets inherited is usually not the shapes but the knowledge that this can be done. Japanese is the clear case: Chinese characters were first used purely for their sound values, a set called man'yōgana, then cursived and clipped — 安 (an) became the hiragana あ (a), and 伊 (i) lost a corner to become the katakana イ (i).

// 02

One sign for one what: how many syllables the language has decides which road is passable

The deepest difference between scripts is not how they look but which layer of unit their signs point at. There are only three roads: point at a phoneme — the smallest sound unit that distinguishes meaning — and you have an alphabet; point at a syllable and you have a syllabary; point at a morpheme, the smallest meaningful unit, and you are on the road Chinese characters took.

Which road is passable is largely fixed by how many syllables the language has. Standard Japanese has only about 100 distinct syllables, so forty-odd basic signs in each of the two kana sets are enough. Most syllables in Cherokee, a North American Indigenous language, are open — a consonant plus a vowel — so 85 signs cover it too.

English cannot go this way. Estimates of how many syllables it permits differ wildly by method, from a few thousand to around thirty thousand; on any of them, one sign per syllable is hopeless, and words like strengths, eight sounds in a single syllable, are everywhere. So it keeps splitting downward to the phoneme layer: two dozen-odd letters cover the sound system of the whole language. Mandarin has fewer than 1,300 distinct syllables counting tone, so a syllabary is in fact passable; characters took the morpheme road instead, and its costs and payoffs were added up in the topic on what makes Chinese unusual.

No script is pure, though. Over 90% of Chinese characters are phono-semantic, with the sound-bearing component sitting there in plain sight; but pronunciation kept shifting for centuries while the components did not, so only around 40% of those components now cue the reading reliably. Conversely, English letters quietly record morphemes: cats and dogs both end in s, yet the first is hissed and the second buzzed like a z. The spelling records the plural morpheme, not the sound of the moment.

So asking whether a script is phonetic or meaning-based gets you nothing. Ask two other things: which layer its signs mostly point at, and how many units that layer has in that particular language.

// 03

A script records only the distinctions its designer could hear

In 1821 a Cherokee man named Sequoyah finished something very hard. He could not read English; he only knew that talk on paper was a real thing. Over more than a decade he picked the syllables of Cherokee apart one at a time and gave each a sign, settling on 85. The uptake was startlingly fast: within a few years a great many people could read and write, and in 1828 the Cherokee Phoenix was printed in parallel Cherokee and English columns.

But the syllabary records two things not at all: Cherokee has tone, and it distinguishes vowel length, and neither is marked on the page. The result is that the words for water and salt are written as the same string of signs — what separates them is exactly vowel length. Someone who speaks the language tells them apart from context at a glance; someone who does not, cannot. This is not a design flaw: Sequoyah built it for people who already spoke Cherokee, and a distinction a native speaker restores on his own need not take up space on the page.

Arabic and Hebrew push the same logic further: standard text does not write short vowels at all, a type of script called an abjad. It works because the meaning lives in the consonants. The three consonants k, t and b own the business of writing — kataba "he wrote", kitāb "book", kātib "writer", maktab "office" — and the vowels that come and go are carrying the grammar and the derivation. Drop them and the root is untouched. English cannot afford the move: strip the vowels from bit, bat, but and bet and all four become bt.

The other extreme records a distinction finer than the phoneme. In the Korean script Hangul, promulgated by King Sejong in 1443, the consonant letters are drawn after the place of articulation: ㄱ draws the back of the tongue rising to block the throat (the sound that starts go), ㄴ draws the tongue tip against the upper gum ridge (the sound that starts no), ㅁ is the square of the lips closing (the sound that starts me). That is a layer below the phoneme — where in the mouth the sound is made. And the alphabet road itself may have been opened only once: essentially every alphabetic script today traces back to the Proto-Sinaitic script, made by Semitic workers in Egypt around four thousand years ago out of borrowed hieroglyphic shapes, with Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Hebrew and Arabic all on that line, and Hangul the acknowledged exception (the family tree is reconstructed from letter shapes and borrowing chains, and particular branches are still argued about).

So writing is not a mirror of language, it is a lossy record: it keeps only the distinctions its designer could hear and judged worth keeping, and leaves the rest for the reader to restore — which the reader manages because he already speaks the language. That is exactly where the topic on reading being an unprepared skill landed: speech grows by itself, literacy does not.

🎒 Scenarios · BigCat

  1. ① Working and living across languages: the same person has three records in your system — the Latin spelling on the passport, the English name he uses himself, the original script. Deduplication misses it, support sees two accounts and opens a third. The mechanism is the one from the section on a script recording only the distinctions its designer could hear: transliteration keeps only the distinctions the target script has slots for, so tone and vowel length — which do distinguish meaning — fall on the floor, and once dropped they cannot be recovered, exactly as the Cherokee words for water and salt are the same string on paper. What to change: make "original script form" its own required field, and let the unique key be a system ID; stop using fuzzy matching on Latin spellings as the only dedup check.
  2. ② Leading a team: the document calls the process "release review", and everyone in the group chat calls it "the meeting". A new hire searches the document's term, finds zero records, concludes nobody has ever done this, and starts a fresh process. Same mechanism, in its time-lapse version: the written layer froze on the day it was signed off, speech kept drifting, and the two drift apart — the drop to around 40% reliability in Chinese phonetic components is this happening over centuries. What to change: put a line under each process name in the document saying what people actually call it, and refresh that alias list every quarter from the terms new hires failed to find; do not try to make everyone standardise their speech, that layer cannot be legislated.

🌀 Crossing over · Interdisciplinary leaps

  • Distributed systems and AI · the Arabic consonant script is already a morpheme-aligned tokenizer: before a large model reads text it cuts it into small pieces called tokens, the business covered in the topic on where a word ends. Run a tokenizer trained on English text over Arabic and a single word can shatter into a dozen pieces, down to individual letters, with the cuts mostly not landing on meaningful boundaries. The mechanism is the one from the section on a script recording only the distinctions its designer could hear: Arabic meaning lives in a three-consonant root (the k-t-b family), and the script happens to leave exactly those consonants on the page while omitting the vowel layer — which is to say the script is itself a morpheme-aligned segmentation, with the lines already drawn. That yields a conclusion you would not otherwise see: the usual tokenizers merge whatever character strings are frequent in the corpus, and in root-and-pattern languages frequent strings and meaningful units are simply not the same thing, so the merging cuts straight through the line the script had already drawn. The testable difference: forbid merges from crossing a morpheme boundary and the gain should be clearly larger for such languages than for English, where affixes are added linearly — people have tried this (MorphBPE reports its biggest gains on Hungarian and Arabic), which is correlational evidence. Where it does not hold is the selection pressure: scripts were filtered under the budget that every child has to learn one within a few years of childhood, so they can only settle on units a speaker can hear for himself; a tokenizer has no learner and is optimising compression over one corpus, while a model with enormous data can reassemble the root out of fragments — a trick a child's data budget cannot pull off.
  • Society and institutions · a sign can be vague, as long as the message space is narrow: the earliest tablets are almost entirely accounts, and lexical lists make up under 1% of the Uruk IV tablets but close to 20% of the Uruk III ones that follow — the range of what people wrote was widening. The mechanism is the rebus step from the section on using the picture of an arrow to write "life": a sloppy sign can be recovered because the reader is guessing inside a very narrow set. A ledger's message space is only "which commodity, how much, whose", so even a crude sign can be identified uniquely; "he did not come yesterday" cannot be guessed, and the only way out is to start spelling the sound. Hence the conclusion: phonetic spelling is not a technical improvement someone thought up, it is forced the moment the message space widens — and it works as a ruler in reverse, since the share of phonetic signs in a script lets you infer how wide the writing had become independently of what the texts say. Chinese happens to have left the graduations: phono-semantic characters run from above 20% of Shang oracle-bone characters to above 80% of seal script to above 90% today. Where it does not hold is the starting point: the Mesoamerican invention did not begin with ledgers. The oldest surviving Zapotec carvings bear calendar day-names, rulers and sacrificed captives. So "writing is born of bookkeeping" is not a law — the narrowness doing the work there was the narrowness of a fixed calendrical and dynastic template.

Going deeper

If you had to design a script today for a language that has none, whose ear should set the distinctions?

Sequoyah's choice shows you can generously drop a pile of distinctions when the users already speak the language; but the same economy badly disadvantages learners, including children of the community who never got the chance to speak it. The two needs genuinely conflict and there is no technically optimal answer, which is why the decision belongs to the speech community.

Could emoji grow into a script?

The missing step is quite specific: they have not gone to recording sound. As long as a sign only points at objects and feelings, what it can express is limited to what can be drawn. To become writing, somebody has to start using them to record pronunciation.

Over 90% of Chinese characters are phono-semantic, yet only around 40% of the components cue the reading reliably. What does that gap mean for a child learning them?

It means "guess the sound from the component" is a strategy that misleads you about half the time, and the child has no way to know in advance which half. It also explains why the entry period for this system is longer, while reading speed afterwards is not at a disadvantage.
