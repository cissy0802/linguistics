Day 4 · Linguistics

Words Are Assembled

You never learned the word wug, but you know what two of them are called — and you picked the right one of three endings
2026-08-31
Show a four-year-old a bird that has never existed, tell them it is a wug, and ask what two of them are called. They say wugs. Nobody taught them that rule; the word is in no dictionary. They did not remember it. They computed it on the spot.

You can do it right now. Here is a word: wug. There is no such thing — a linguist made it up in 1958 for an experiment. What are two of them called? You barely had to think.

The more interesting part is the sound at the end. The English plural has three pronunciations: cats ends in a crisp s (the hiss in bus), dogs ends in a voiced z (your throat buzzing, like a bee), and glasses adds a whole extra syllable. Nobody ever drilled you on how to choose, and you have never chosen wrong — not even for a word that does not exist.

Which means words are not pulled whole out of the dictionary in your head. They are assembled on the spot. This piece is about that assembly line: what the parts are, how many ways there are to join them, and which half of the sentence "Chinese has no morphology" is actually right.

// 01

You can pluralize a word that does not exist

First, a name for the parts. A morpheme is the smallest piece that still carries meaning. Unhappiness splits into three: un (negation), happy, and ness (the part that turns an adjective into a noun). Below that, nothing is left to split — the u in un means nothing at all.

The test for splitting is not "looks like a piece." In cranberry, the berry part is obviously a morpheme; what about cran? It has no independent meaning and appears in no other word. These are common enough that linguistics named them: cranberry morphemes. Their existence tells you what the criterion really is — a chunk counts as a part if it can be reused somewhere else.

Now the experiment. In 1958 Jean Berko drew a small blue creature, told children aged four to seven "this is a wug," turned the page to show two of them, and read the fragment "Now there are two ___" for the child to complete. The children said wugs. That drawing became the most reproduced picture in the field.

The trick is that wug does not exist: no dictionary, no parent, no exposure. If a child's head held a list of words, they would stall. They did not stall, which means what they hold is a rule that runs on any stem at all.

They also picked the right ending. The three pronunciations divide the work precisely: after a voiceless consonant you get the s sound (cats), after a voiced one you get z (dogs, wugs), and after hissing sounds like s, z or sh you add an entire syllable (glasses). Nobody states this division out loud, and four-year-olds obey it.

The unevenness carries the most information. Children are near ceiling on the first two branches and collapse on the one that needs an extra syllable: for Berko's invented word gutch (plural gutches), only 28% of four- and five-year-olds produced it, and only 38% of the five-and-a-half to seven group — while the same children were at 91% on the real word glasses. So what a child learns is not "the plural rule" but several sub-rules covering different territory, the frequent ones first and the rare ones years later.

Evidence strength: a single classic experiment, but replicated endlessly. When the design is exported to other languages, one precondition is easy to miss — the language must have an obligatory inflectional category to test in the first place. Japanese nouns do not obligatorily mark plurality, so the same materials transplanted directly leave nothing to measure. That is not a fact about Japanese; it is a limit on the ruler.

// 02

One kind of assembly makes new words, the other just changes clothes

Put teacher and teachers side by side. Teach plus er built a new word and even changed its class (verb to noun); it earns a dictionary entry. The s in teachers built nothing — it is the same word wearing different clothes for a different slot in the sentence, and no dictionary lists it separately.

The first is derivation, the second inflection. Telling them apart is not a matter of feel; there are four checkable properties.

First, whether identity changes. Happy to happiness changes word class, so it is derivation; happy to happier stays an adjective, so it is inflection. Second, order. Teach-er-s works, teach-s-er does not — inflection always sits on the outermost layer. Third, holes. Inflection has almost none: every English verb has a past tense, including one you invented a minute ago. Derivation is full of them: arrive gives arrival, but not arrivement, even though ment is plainly alive (government, movement); and th (warm to warmth, wide to width) retired long ago. Fourth, whether the meaning is predictable. Teachers is always "more than one teacher," without a single exception; transmission can mean sending something, or the gearbox in your car — a derived word grows its own meaning and then keeps it.

How these two kinds of assembly get packaged varies enormously across languages. The Turkish evlerimden on the left of the figure is a single word, one bead per meaning, in a fixed order, like stringing a necklace — that is agglutinative packaging. The Latin amō in the middle goes the other way: the ō at the end does four jobs at once, and you cannot slice it into a person part plus a tense part. That is fusional.

Inflection has one further property that matters far more than whether a language has it: you cannot opt out. In English, the moment you mention a countable noun you are forced to choose between singular and plural, even when the information is useless here, even when you do not know how many there are. Jakobson put it best in 1959: languages differ essentially in what they must convey, not in what they may convey. The next section is built entirely on that sentence. And to be clear: agglutinative, fusional and barely-packaged are not rungs on a ladder. All three convey the same content; they just put the complexity in different places — inside the word, or out in the word order and the function words.

// 03

When people say Mandarin has no morphology, which half is right

You have probably heard the claim. Half of it holds, and the half that gets dropped is the more interesting one.

The half that holds: inflection really is sparse. In 我昨天去 (wǒ zuótiān qù, "I went yesterday") and 他明天去 (tā míngtiān qù, "he goes tomorrow"), the verb 去 (qù, "go") is simply 去 (qù), unchanged. Nouns are not obliged to mark number either: the 书 (shū, "book") in 一本书 (yī běn shū, "one book") and in 三本书 (sān běn shū, "three books") is identical. The "cannot opt out" from the last section can be opted out of here — a Mandarin speaker can go a whole sentence without revealing how many, and an English speaker cannot.

But sparse is not absent. Mandarin has 们 (-men), as in 学生们 (xuéshengmen, "students") and 朋友们 (péngyoumen, "friends"). It comes with hard limits: it attaches essentially only to nouns denoting people, and it cannot combine with a numeral-classifier phrase — 三个学生们 (sān ge xuéshengmen, "three students-PL") strikes most native speakers as off. It is not obligatory inflection but a conditional, optional marker.

The half that gets dropped: word-building is extremely active. Compounding does most of the work — 电 (diàn, "electricity") plus 脑 (nǎo, "brain") gives 电脑 (diànnǎo, "computer"); 手 (shǒu, "hand") plus 机 (jī, "machine") gives 手机 (shǒujī, "mobile phone"). There is a set of half-bleached parts too: 性 (-xìng, roughly "-ity"), 化 (-huà, roughly "-ize"), 员 (-yuán, "staff member"). And there is reduplication — 看 (kàn, "look") becomes 看看 (kànkan, "have a quick look"), lighter and briefer. All of this is words being assembled; it just happens on the word-building layer rather than the inflectional one.

The category Mandarin genuinely will not let you skip sits elsewhere: the classifier. When you count things you cannot escape it. You cannot say 三书 (sān shū); you must say 三本书 (sān běn shū, "three books"), and you must pick the right one from a large set — 本 (běn, for bound volumes), 张 (zhāng, for flat things), 条 (tiáo, for long thin things), 只 (zhī, for many animals). This is exactly the "forces you to be explicit" from the last section, landing in a different place: English forces you to declare quantity, Mandarin forces you to declare shape or kind.

So Mandarin-speaking children have their own hurdle. They do not learn plural agreement; they learn classifiers, and the observed path looks like the wug story in reverse: first the general 个 (gè, the all-purpose classifier) is used for everything (三个鸟 sān ge niǎo, "three birds"; 三个纸 sān ge zhǐ, "three papers"), then it slowly differentiates. Studies report roughly a third of children using nothing but 个 (gè) throughout a production task, with individual classifiers still incomplete at ten and a dip-then-recovery U-shape along the way. This is the same thing as an English child saying goed — grab the cheapest general rule first, patch in the exceptions later.

Two things should be flagged honestly. First, whether 了 (le), 着 (zhe) and 过 (guò) count as affixes has been argued for a long time — they behave like inflection (fixed position after the verb) and like detachable function words, with no settled answer. Second, whether the unit "word" is even clear in Chinese is also contested: early small studies (Hoosain 1992, and the manual annotation comparisons Sproat and colleagues ran in 1996 while building a segmenter) reported substantial disagreement among native speakers about where words end; a large crowdsourced study in 2017 obtained proportionate agreement of 0.91 to 0.93 and concluded the opposite. Evidence strength: the later study has a far bigger sample, but its metric is per-position proportionate agreement, and since the vast majority of positions are obviously not boundaries, that number runs high by construction.

So the accurate version of the claim is: Mandarin has very little inflectional morphology. Information can be encoded in the shape of a word (that English s) or somewhere else entirely (Mandarin's classifiers, word order, time adverbs). That is a division of labour, not a ranking — every language forces you to be explicit about something, just in a different place.

🎒 In Practice · BigCat

  1. ① Writing and communication: someone in a code review asks whether the normalize() function, the normalization block in the config, and the normalized field in the response are the same thing — and twenty minutes later it turns out they are three unrelated things. That is not sloppy naming; it is the mechanism from "one kind of assembly makes new words, the other just changes clothes" biting you. A derived form is a new word, and its meaning drifts on its own (transmission becoming a gearbox), while readers default to assuming a shared stem means a shared referent. Two things to change: add a naming rule that within one module a single stem may carry business meaning in only one derived form, and any second one must switch stems; and grep for duplicate names by stem, not by full identifier — full-string matching will never surface this class of collision.
  2. ② Working and living across languages: anyone who has spent time in a German- or Russian-speaking environment has hit this wall — you hear a word, look it up, and it is not there, because dictionaries list the bare stem while what you heard was wrapped in two more layers. The mechanism is simply "inflection always sits on the outermost layer." What to change is the daily study action: stop memorizing new entries and instead take real text and drill "hear an unfamiliar form, report its dictionary form," with that recovery accuracy as the metric; and switch to lookup tools that do stemming. A slow-growing vocabulary is fine — an entry you cannot recover is an entry you cannot use.

🌀 Crossing Over · Connections Beyond the Field

  • Distributed systems and AI · whether the frequency effect attaches to the rule or to the entry: someone took Berko's materials and gave them to a large language model. Weissweiler and colleagues did this in 2023 across English, German, Tamil and Turkish, and found ChatGPT falling well short of purpose-built morphological systems (most visibly in English), with a "real word bias" in all four languages — the same rule applied to an existing word comes out far more accurate than the same rule applied to an invented one. The mechanism at work is the one from "you can pluralize a word that does not exist": if a rule is attached to structure, whether the stem really exists should make no difference. But the useful part of this is not "models are worse than people." It is a testable difference: humans have a frequency effect too, just in a different place. In Berko's own data children fall from 91% to 28%, and what varies is not how familiar the stem is but how often that sub-rule shows up in the language; when the model falls, what varies is how many times that word form appeared in training. So the test is concrete: build a batch of entirely new stems, half taking a common morphological pattern, half taking a rare but perfectly regular one. Human scores should track the frequency of the pattern and ignore the stem; if a model tracks the entry instead, the two curves separate right there. Where this breaks down: measuring a difference is not the same as showing the mechanisms differ. A large enough model could learn sensitivity to patterns, at which point the curves converge — and that would still not prove a rule is what sits inside. The experiment can falsify "the model only memorizes entries." It cannot establish "the model learned the rule."
  • Society and institutions · a required field being full does not make it accurate: the deadly property of inflection is that you cannot opt out — an English speaker is forced to choose singular or plural on every countable noun, even with no idea how many there are. Carry that to forms and ticketing systems and you get a counterintuitive conclusion: a field's fill rate and its information content are unrelated, and often run opposite. A required field is 100% filled by construction, and precisely because it cannot be skipped, its discriminative power may be near zero; an optional field, once someone chooses to fill it, carries the signal that someone bothered. The metric to switch to is each field's mutual information with the outcome you actually care about, not completeness. Where this breaks down is the most useful part: linguistic obligation is costless and unconscious — nobody utters a "lazy plural" to save effort, so the values of grammatical categories stay trustworthy. Form obligation has friction, and people enter garbage to get past it. The difference is visible: grammatical number tracks reality, while a required field's values pile up into an unnatural spike on some default option. Once you see that spike, the field has already stopped working.

Going Deeper

If the line between inflection and derivation is so clear, why are there always cases nobody can settle?

English ing is sometimes inflection (he is running) and sometimes derivation (good running shoes, or even a noun, the running of the race). Is that one part in two uses, or two parts that happen to sound alike? Which criterion decides — order, holes, or predictability of meaning? And if the three criteria disagree, what does that tell you?

Is "cannot opt out" a fact about the language or about the speaker?

An English speaker is forced to mark number, but can say some and leave the quantity vague. So is the obligation to supply the information, or merely to run through the choice? If it is only the latter, how much weight does Jakobson's line lose?

Can you measure how alive a language's word-building actually is?

The intuitive move is to count how many words a part has produced, but that is historical stock. The th suffix has produced a decent number (warmth, width, depth, length) and yet makes no new ones. Would counting its appearances among the last decade's new words be more accurate? Is that metric fair to a part that was itself only just invented?
