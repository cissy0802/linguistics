Day 14 · Linguistics

Poverty of the Stimulus, or Usage-Based?

Nobody hands a child a rule book, and almost nobody tells him which sentences he may not say — so how does he get the unheard ones right?
2026-09-10
Sixty years of argument, and the two sides have never been arguing about whether language is learned. Both agree that it is. What they argue about is whether the material a child gets is enough — and lately "enough" has finally become a number you can compute.

You understood that last sentence, but not because you had met it before. You almost certainly read it for the first time just now, and whoever wrote it did not pull it out of memory either. Everyone produces and understands sentences that have never existed before, all day long, and a two-year-old has already started doing the same thing.

The hard part is what he has to work with: only the speech around him. No textbook, no table of rules, and hardly anyone correcting him line by line when he gets something wrong. One side says that isn't enough, so something must come pre-installed. The other side says it is enough, and that we have underestimated how much information that speech carries while overestimating how abstract a young child's grammar is to begin with.

Three things this time: what the "not enough" side offers as evidence, what the "enough" side answers with, and what shape the argument has taken today.

// 01

A question he had never heard, and he got it right the first time

To turn an English statement into a yes/no question, you move something: the is in "The man is tall" goes to the front, and you get "Is the man tall?"

Lengthen the sentence and the trouble starts. "The man who is beating the donkey is mean" has two instances of is. Which one moves?

One rule counts by position: front the first is you come to. That rule is far simpler — you need no notion of a clause, just left-to-right counting. It gives you "Is the man who beating the donkey is mean", a sentence any English speaker sees is wrong at a glance.

The other rule has to cut the sentence into chunks first: "the man who is beating the donkey" is one whole chunk, the part that says who; the is that moves is the one outside that chunk. That gives "Is the man who is beating the donkey mean", the right one. This property — the rule looks at structure, not at position — is called structure dependence.

How do we know they don't take it? In 1987 Crain and Nakayama sat thirty three-to-five-year-olds in front of a puppet and asked them to put questions to it: "Ask Jabba if the man who is beating the donkey is mean." The children made all sorts of errors — most often saying is twice — but not one of them produced the error the counting rule predicts.

That is the shape of the argument known as the poverty of the stimulus: children draw distinctions that go beyond what the material they heard can support, so where does the extra come from?

Two parts of that foundation have since loosened. First, the corpora: Pullum and Scholz went and counted in 2002 and found the crucial sentence type is not absent at all, running to something like one percent of the interrogatives in a typical corpus; Legate and Yang replied the same year that what matters is the speech children actually hear, where it is rare to the point of near-absence. Second, the experiment: Ambridge, Rowland and Pine redid it in 2008 with modals like can and should and with plural are in place of is — and children did produce the counting-rule error.

So the accurate statement is not "children never make this error" but "the error is rare, and it depends on which auxiliary you use". Strength of evidence: the original result has been replicated, but change one word and the conclusion moves. One more caveat: a Mandarin yes/no question moves nothing at all — you add the particle 吗 (ma, a sentence-final question marker) and you are done. The puzzle simply does not arise in that language, and the evidence above is evidence English happens to supply.

// 02

A child's grammar starts out one verb at a time

The other side comes in not through a syntactic puzzle but through a diary. Tomasello recorded his daughter's verbs day by day in 1992 and found that within a single week she could use one verb in several different frames while the one she had just picked up came in only one. What she had was not "how to build a sentence" but "how cut works", "how draw works" — one island per word, which is why this is called the verb island pattern. The whole position on this side is called usage-based: structure is an abstraction slowly ground out of particular utterances, not a frame that words get dropped into.

In 1999 Akhtar ran a clean test of it. She taught two-, three- and four-year-olds a made-up verb, demonstrating it in a deliberately odd word order — English normally runs agent, verb, patient, and she used orders like agent, patient, verb. Then she brought out new puppets and asked what was happening. About half the two-year-olds reproduced the odd order. The four-year-olds almost all corrected it to English order. Word order is not learned in one shot; it accumulates verb by verb.

But this side took a hit too. In 2006 Gertner, Fisher and Eisengart switched to a looking task: twenty-one-month-olds heard "The duck is gorping the bunny" — gorp being a word they had never met — while one screen showed a duck pushing a bunny and the other a bunny pulling a duck. They looked longer at the first. On word order alone, they had sorted out who was doing what to whom with a brand-new verb.

One result says abstraction comes late, the other that it comes early, and they are not measuring the same thing: one asks a child to produce a sentence, the other only to look a moment longer. Comprehension runs ahead, production trails, and the gap between them is now a research topic in its own right.

More interesting still, the picture changes when you change language. Dittmar and colleagues tested German children in 2008. German marks who-does-what not only by order but by case marking — articles and endings that flag the agent — and its word order is looser than English. Two-year-olds understood only when order and case pointed the same way; five-year-olds could go on order alone; only at seven did they, like adults, trust case when the two cues conflicted. What gets abstracted, and how fast, tracks which cue that particular language makes reliable. Strength of evidence: these are replicated laboratory results, and the disagreement is about interpretation, not about the data.

// 03

They no longer argue whether, they argue how much

The real change in this argument is not that somebody won; it is that the question changed shape. It used to be "is syntactic knowledge innate", which no data can settle. Now it is: how strong a bias does a learner need in order to arrive at structure dependence from roughly the amount of material a child gets, and is that bias specific to language or the sort any sequence learner has? Both versions can be run on a computer.

Perfors, Tenenbaum and Regier did one side in 2011. They fed real child-directed speech to an ideal Bayesian learner and let it choose between grammars of the counting kind and grammars of the chunking kind. It chose the latter — not because a preference had been installed, but because on this data chunking pays better overall: a slightly more complicated set of rules that explains the corpus far more economically. In that setting, at least, "the material cannot settle it" is false. The discount to apply: the set of candidate grammars was supplied in advance by the researchers.

Yedetore, Linzen, Frank and McCoy did the other side in 2023, and did it closer to life. Instead of an ideal learner they used two kinds of network with no built-in preference for hierarchy — a recurrent one and a Transformer — trained on child-directed text comparable in quantity and content to what a child hears. The result splits in two: the models captured the surface statistics well, and then generalised by the counting rule as soon as they had to form questions.

Scaling up did not overturn that. Lan, Chemla and Katzir moved the test onto six models in 2024 — two recurrent networks and four Transformers, including the general-purpose large models public at the time — probing a finer phenomenon in English wh-movement. They set a fairly lenient bar for success, and the models still often failed it.

So the state of play is this: on the same material, an ideal learner picks out structure dependence and actual networks do not. Where the difference lies is an experiment you can run, not a position to hold. Neither side gets a free step here — the models get text only, with no intonation, no scene, no back-and-forth feedback, and a child has all three; reading the models' failure as evidence for human innateness and reading their success as "so nothing need be innate" are the same unearned step.

The usage-based side has been making itself quantitative too. Children produce sentences nobody says to them, like "Don't giggle me", and then quietly withdraw them over a few years. The withdrawal runs on two things: preemption, hearing other people repeatedly use a different form in that same slot, and entrenchment, one form being heard so often that it squeezes its competitors out. Both are functions of frequency, so you can compute a prediction from a corpus and then go test it.

🎒 In Context · BigCat

  1. ① Leading a team: in code review you point out a problem, and the person fixes that one spot; in the next commit it reappears in a different file, and you point it out again. After three rounds you start putting it down to carelessness. The mechanism in "A child's grammar starts out one verb at a time" offers another reading: abstraction has to grow out of several different items — in Akhtar's study half the two-year-olds went along with the odd word order, and only by four did nearly all of them correct it; what was missing was items seen, not understanding. So stop asking "does that make sense?", which probes the comprehension side, and comprehension runs ahead anyway. Ask instead for a second instance the person finds themselves, in a file you never mentioned. If they can't, pointing out a third spot won't generalise either: what's owed is a second example, not a third reminder.
  2. ② Writing: you send a draft to a colleague and most of what comes back is "looks good", with the occasional "I might phrase this differently". Which sentence you shouldn't have written that way, almost nobody tells you outright. That is exactly the child's situation. In "They no longer argue whether, they argue how much", the usage-based side's mechanism for it is preemption: not somebody correcting you, but you seeing other people use a different form in that same slot, over and over, until yours gets squeezed out. Two things to change: replace "can you see anything wrong with this?" with "how would you write this paragraph?" — you want a substitute, not a verdict; and replace "read more good writing" with reading the same slot across the same genre — the opening line of comparable emails, the closing section of comparable design docs. Reading widely doesn't supply that alignment.

🌀 Crossing Over · Notes from Other Fields

  • Distributed systems and AI · Same data, two generalisations, and the gap may not be about how much data: train a network with no preference for hierarchy on child-directed speech and it captures the surface statistics beautifully, then generalises by counting position as soon as it forms questions. What is running is the mechanism from "A question he had never heard, and he got it right the first time": that material is compatible with both rules at once, so which one you land on is fixed by inductive bias, not by the material. From this follows a difference you can actually measure, rather than one more remark about whether models resemble people. If the gap comes from quantity, scaling the corpus should produce an inflection — past some size the model flips to the chunking rule. If it comes from bias, scaling only fits the counting rule better: a monotone curve that never flips. Those two predictions look nothing alike, and one curve separates them. Where it breaks down is the assumption that a model's input equals a child's. Text has had intonation stripped out, and in English the boundary between a main clause and an embedded one often carries a pause and a pitch change — precisely the external marking of which stretch is one chunk. The scene, the other person's gaze, their reaction when you get it wrong: none of that is in the text either. To make the argument stand you would have to put the prosodic marking back and test the same thing; until then the failure only shows that text alone is not enough.
  • Complexity science · A threshold line: how many exceptions before a rule stops generalising: word frequencies have a long tail — a few words take most of the occurrences, and the vast majority you meet only a handful of times in your life. Under that distribution, "I have never heard it" barely licenses "it is not allowed". What is running is the mechanism from "A child's grammar starts out one verb at a time": rules grow out of particular items, and whether one grows depends on the ratio of rule-followers to exceptions. Charles Yang wrote that as a number: for a category of N words, the rule stops generalising once the exceptions exceed N divided by the natural log of N. What follows is not the platitude that too many exceptions block learning, but a nonlinear consequence — at N of 9 the rule tolerates 4 exceptions, forty-four percent of the class; at N of 1000 it tolerates only 145, under fifteen percent. Bigger categories tolerate exceptions less well, so the same irregular form survives in a small class and eventually gets flattened by the rule in a large one, which points language change in a computable direction. In artificial-language experiments, five- and six-year-olds fall almost dichotomously on the two sides of that line. Where it breaks down is the assumption that N is known. The line requires you to know in advance which words the rule is supposed to cover, but a real vocabulary is open and long-tailed: N keeps moving as you meet new words, and you never get to do the arithmetic in a final state. Using it as a stable critical value assumes more than it looks like it does.

Thinking Further

If the counting-rule error does show up under some conditions, what is left of the poverty of the stimulus argument?

One reading is that it has been falsified. Another is that it retreats from "never occurs" to "occurs far less than chance", and the second still needs explaining. Different next steps separate the two: the first needs only a few more attested errors, the second needs a baseline — if children went purely by position, how many bad questions should there be? What you compute that baseline from is itself hard.

German children trust case only at seven, English children use word order at twenty-one months. Is that a difference between languages or between tasks?

The worrying possibility is that it is neither — the two groups had different tasks, different materials, different labs. What would have to be equated for the cross-linguistic comparison to mean anything? And if a difference survived the equating, would you attribute it to how reliable each cue is, or to where each cue sits (case on the word ending, order at the front of the sentence)?

What else should differ between a model that learns structure dependence and one that doesn't, beyond this one phenomenon?

If a preference for hierarchy is really one thing, it shouldn't surface only in questions. Think about where else you have to cut chunks before you can operate: what a pronoun refers to, how far a negation reaches, the requirement that two coordinated items be the same kind of thing. A proposal that holds only for the phenomenon it was tested on is usually measuring that phenomenon, not the proposal.
